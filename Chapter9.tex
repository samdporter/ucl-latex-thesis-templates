\chapter{Discussion, Future Work \& Conclusion}
\label{discussion}

\section{Discussion}

The results answer the thesis question in a qualified sense. Post-\gls{sirt} \gls{y90} imaging improved when PET, SPECT and CT were used together during reconstruction, but the gain depended on three parts of the pipeline: the Bremsstrahlung SPECT forward model, the form of the inter-modality coupling, and the optimisation of the resulting objective. Treating any one of these in isolation was insufficient.

One of the clearest results is that anatomical guidance and functional coupling are complementary rather than interchangeable. In the bootstrapped \gls{nema} study of Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}, \dtv{} (directional guidance without coupling) and \wtnv{} (coupling without directional guidance) produced broadly similar PET appearances and similar recovery, and neither recovered the smallest spheres at the matched operating point. Recovery improved significantly only when both mechanisms acted together, for all sphere sizes $\leq\SI{22}{\milli\metre}$. This matches the intended mechanism of \wdtnv: the higher-count SPECT image provides functional support for the location of activity transitions, while the $\mu$-derived direction field determines which gradients are penalised there. Neither source of side information was sufficient on its own in this count regime. That matters for the design of related coupled priors, not just for this application.

The deconvolution study of Chapter~\ref{chapter:deconvolution_pvc} showed the same complementarity in a simpler setting and exposed a separate problem for quantification. \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} have no explicit penalty, so the stopping iteration becomes part of the regularisation. The sphere study therefore used ground-truth \gls{nrmse} to choose the stopping point, while the brain example relied on visual assessment. Neither rule is available in a clinical reconstruction. The resulting estimate depends on the iteration and, because the iterates retain information from their starting point, on the initialising image. \gls{rl}, \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} all changed when the initialisation changed; with the blurred \gls{pet} initialisation, the best unregularised \gls{rl} result occurred at iteration zero. \gls{rldtv}, in contrast, reached the same optima under both initialisations and achieved the lowest \gls{nrmse}, the highest aggregate \gls{rc} and the lowest background \gls{cov}.

The same issue appears in the full reconstruction. \gls{shkem} in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} is evaluated at a selected subiteration chosen to approximately match the vendor reconstruction's background \gls{cov}. \wdtnv{}, by contrast, is iterated towards the minimiser of a fixed objective, with the operating point set by the regularisation weights. Both approaches require a choice, but the penalised formulation makes that choice part of the model rather than part of the stopping rule. That makes it easier to transfer between datasets and separates it from the optimiser, as the experiments in Chapter~\ref{chapter:optimisation} demonstrate. The deconvolution study also distinguishes two uses of anatomical guidance: \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} build it into the kernelised image model, whereas \gls{rldtv} uses it in an explicit regularisation term. The same distinction appears at the smallest sphere sizes in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}, where \wdtnv{} separates from \gls{shkem}.

CT was used for direction rather than as a third intensity channel. Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} keeps those two roles separate: $\Pi_{m,n}$ supplies anatomical direction and $B_n$ handles modality scale. The voxel-wise Jacobian therefore remains two-dimensional, so the spectral calculation reduces to a $2\times2$ inverse square root and remains affordable at clinical image sizes. The trade-off is explicit. CT contributes orientation but not contrast, while the prior avoids transferring CT contrast into the emission images.

Chapter~\ref{chapterlabel3} made that coupling safer to use. A joint prior shares whatever each channel contains, including systematic modelling error, so coupling a poorly modelled Bremsstrahlung SPECT channel to a count-limited PET channel could propagate bias in both directions. The under-relaxed, bounded reconstruct--simulate refinement and the calibrated effective range kernel produced a SPECT model that was stable enough for the joint study. This also sets a higher standard for each individual forward model: synergy cannot compensate for an acquisition model that is wrong in a systematic way.

The optimisation results were not just an implementation detail. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:optimisation}, prior-aware preconditioning was necessary for the count-limited PET channel: data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling diverged under the tested step-size schedule, while all four prior-curvature models remained bounded and continued to reduce regional error. SPECT was more robust, and data-only scaling remained competitive there. Thus the same prior produced different effective conditioning in the two channels. The four curvature models behaved similarly, so the practical conclusion is simple: the preconditioner must include the prior. The subset experiment points to a related result. How often the prior was applied mattered more than how the data subsets were grouped, and only the folded schedule converged at matched average prior weight.

In the clinical cohort, \wdtnv{} yielded significantly higher lesion \gls{tbr} than \dtv, \wtnv{} and \gls{shkem} across 45 lesions in nine patients at approximately matched background \gls{cov}. The margin over \gls{shkem} was the smallest, which is unsurprising because \gls{shkem} uses CT-informed kernel features and is a strong comparator. In the phantom, it matched \wdtnv{} for spheres of \SI{22}{\milli\metre} and above and separated only at \SI{13}{\milli\metre} and \SI{10}{\milli\metre}. Those are the sizes the prior was intended to improve. There was a reproducibility trade-off: at the matched operating point, the more strongly smoothing \dtv{} and \wtnv{} baselines had lower voxel-wise \gls{cov} than \wdtnv{}.

Lesion-level responses were heterogeneous. Lesions with clear anatomical correlates, such as adjacent calcification or a liver capsule boundary, benefited most; lesions without a corresponding structure in the $\mu$-map improved less. This is the intended behaviour of the prior. CTAC guidance steers orientation preferences where anatomy is present and leaves the functional data to determine the result where it is not. Since the directional weighting field $\|\xi\|$ is available before reconstruction, it could also provide a practical indication of which lesions are likely to benefit once they have been identified on a routine clinical reconstruction.

The clinical case for joint reconstruction does not depend on either modality being unusable. PET- and SPECT-based mean absorbed doses are highly correlated across liver \glspl{voi} \cite{kappadathQuantitativeEvaluation90YPET2024}, but the same comparison reports a systematic offset: PET gives, on average, a $14\%$ higher tumour mean dose and a lower perfused-normal-liver mean dose than SPECT. That lesion-level difference is consistent with the different resolution and partial-volume behaviour of the two modalities, and it is the kind of difference reconstruction can change. As dosimetry-guided prescription for \gls{sirt} becomes more formalised \cite{salemClinicalDosimetricConsiderations2019} and dosimetry is more widely implemented \cite{gearEANMEnablingGuide2023}, lesion-level activity estimates will matter more. Higher scanner sensitivity may reduce the severity of count limitation for PET at some centres, but it will not remove the modelling difficulties specific to Bremsstrahlung SPECT.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion_limitations}

The most practically significant limitation is the sensitivity of the directional field to CTAC quality. Because $\xi$ is derived from $D\mu$, coherent CT artefacts such as arms-down streaking are reproduced in $\|\xi\|$ and provide a pathway for artefact transfer into the emission reconstructions, as observed in the training patient of Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}. The stabiliser $\eta$ limits amplification of small gradients but cannot suppress structured, high-contrast artefacts. This is a property of deriving guidance from the raw attenuation map rather than of the prior itself, and it is addressable; where a low-dose CTAC is used for guidance, acquisition with arms raised where feasible is preferable. Relatedly, the sensitivity of \wdtnv{} to residual PET/SPECT/CT registration error was not characterised. A prior that rewards aligned edges is, by construction, the class of method most exposed to misregistration, so this deserves explicit study.

The evaluation protocol also carries limitations that follow from the decision to preserve direct comparability with \gls{shkem}. Regularisation weights were selected once on a single training patient and then fixed, which avoids repeated tuning on the evaluation cohort but does not establish the robustness of those weights across acquisition protocols and lesion presentations. Quantitative endpoints were restricted to PET, so the jointly reconstructed SPECT images, although visually less noisy while retaining lesion conspicuity, were assessed qualitatively only. The endpoints themselves are image-quality surrogates: recovery, \gls{tbr} and \gls{cov} are the quantities that reconstruction most directly influences, but the clinical motivation is absorbed dose, and no dose-level or outcome-level analysis was performed. Finally, the clinical cohort is single-centre and retrospective, and the thesis compares against variational and kernel-based methods but not against learned reconstruction or learned correction approaches \cite{hashimotoPETImageReconstruction2022,jia90YSPECTScatter2023}, which are the most likely external competitors for this problem.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

\subsection{Relative Nuclear Variation}
\label{subsec:future_rnv}

The modality weights used by \wdtnv{} compensate for the markedly different global scales of the PET and SPECT images, but do not adapt to spatial variation in activity within either image. One possible extension, inspired by the \acrfull{rdp} \cite{nuytsConcavePriorPenalizing2002}, would scale the coupled edge magnitude by a local activity measure. Let $r_n(\bm{z})=\|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n(\bm{z})\|_*$ denote the nuclear magnitude of the weighted directional Jacobian used by \wdtnv{}, and let $s_n(\bm{z})\geq0$ denote a weighted local activity sum over the same finite-difference neighbourhood. A proposed \acrfull{rnv} penalty is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{RNV}}(\bm{z})
    :=
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{n=1}^{N}
    \frac{r_n(\bm{z})^2}
    {s_n(\bm{z})+\gamma_{\mathrm{RNV}}r_n(\bm{z})
        +\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RNV}}},
    \qquad
    \gamma_{\mathrm{RNV}}>0,\quad
    \varepsilon_{\mathrm{RNV}}>0.
    \label{eq:rnv_prior}
\end{equation}
The construction is ``relative'' because the penalty on a coupled edge is moderated by the local activity scale. For fixed non-negative modality weights on the non-negative image domain, the scalar density $r^2/(s+\gamma_{\mathrm{RNV}}r+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RNV}})$ is jointly convex in $(r,s)$ and non-decreasing in $r$; since $r_n$ is convex and $s_n$ is affine, the proposed penalty is convex under these assumptions. This construction was not implemented or evaluated in this thesis, and whether such local scaling improves the bias--noise trade-off without weakening cross-modality regularisation inappropriately remains to be established.

\subsection{Robustness and Clinical Translation}
\label{subsec:future_translation}

The first methodological priority is to make the directional field less sensitive to CTAC quality. Estimating $\xi$ from a denoised, artefact-corrected or segmentation-derived attenuation map, rather than from $D\mu$ alone, would preserve the anatomical orientation information while reducing the route by which coherent streak artefacts enter the emission reconstruction. Registration error is a second unresolved issue. Controlled rigid and deformable perturbations of the SPECT-to-PET warp would show how much lesion-level bias the prior introduces; if that effect is material, a joint reconstruction--registration formulation would be the natural extension. The optimisation study raises a third, more practical question: one shared step-size schedule was not equally well calibrated to both modalities. Increasing the initial step improved SPECT convergence but destabilised count-limited PET, so modality-dependent initial steps or relaxation rates are worth testing.

The framework could also be tested beyond the favourable case in which PET and SPECT image the same underlying \gls{y90} microsphere distribution. Shared anatomy with non-identical functional distributions, including \gls{maa} planning versus post-treatment imaging, multi-nuclide studies, or multiple timepoints, would require weaker or spatially adaptive coupling so that modality-specific or evolving uptake is not suppressed. The final step is to move from image-quality endpoints to absorbed dose. Voxel-level dose kernels, dose--volume measures, and a multi-centre cohort with response data would test whether the lesion-level improvements translate into more reliable dosimetry.

Two comparisons would help place the framework more clearly. The effective range kernel of Chapter~\ref{chapterlabel3} is isotropic and water-equivalent; an anisotropic, material-dependent Bremsstrahlung model such as that of Lim \textit{et al.} \cite{lim_y-90_2018} would show how much residual SPECT modelling error comes from that approximation. The thesis also compares variational and kernel-based methods, but not learned reconstruction or learned correction methods \cite{hashimotoPETImageReconstruction2022,jia90YSPECTScatter2023}. Those comparisons would show where an interpretable, physically constrained prior sits relative to current data-driven alternatives.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:thesis_conclusion}

This thesis set out to improve quantitative post-\gls{sirt} \gls{y90} imaging by using PET, SPECT and CT together during reconstruction rather than independently, and to do so in a form that remains tractable at clinical image sizes. Four contributions support that aim.

First, Chapter~\ref{chapterlabel3} established the Bremsstrahlung SPECT model components required before the two emission modalities can safely be coupled. A domain-shifted learned projection estimate was used to initialise object- and acquisition-specific SIMIND simulation of non-primary counts; an under-relaxed update remained bounded over the refinement budget where full replacement did not; and a calibrated effective Gaussian range kernel brought projected response widths substantially closer to GATE and SIMIND across three energy windows. The resulting model was frozen and carried forward unchanged.

Second, Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} introduced \wdtnv, a smoothed, weighted and directional form of \gls{tnv}. Charbonnier smoothing of the singular values yields a differentiable spectral functional whose Jacobian-space gradient can be written in Gram-matrix form, reducing the key spectral operation for two coupled emission modalities to a $2\times2$ inverse square root and removing the need for a per-voxel \gls{svd}. The formulation separates anatomical direction, applied through $\Pi_{m,n}$, from modality scale, applied through $B_n$, so that CT steers the penalty without entering as a coupled intensity channel. The design principle behind that choice was established beforehand in the controlled deconvolution setting of Chapter~\ref{chapter:deconvolution_pvc}, where an explicit directional penalty outperformed kernelised image models and, unlike them, gave solutions that depended on neither the initialisation nor a ground-truth-informed stopping rule.

Third, Chapter~\ref{chapter:optimisation} treated the optimisation of the coupled objective as part of the contribution rather than as an implementation concern. The PET bed positions are reconstructed jointly, so that the edge-preserving prior acts consistently across their overlap instead of being applied independently before fusion. With the average prior weight per data pass matched, the prior-application schedule mattered more than the subset organisation, and only the folded schedule converged. Prior-aware preconditioning was required for stable convergence of the count-limited PET channel, where data-only scaling diverged, although the four prior curvature models examined behaved similarly to one another.

Fourth, Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} evaluated the complete framework on \gls{y90} data. On a bootstrapped \gls{nema} phantom, \wdtnv{} significantly improved recovery of spheres of \SI{22}{\milli\metre} and below relative to \dtv{} and \wtnv, and improved on \gls{shkem} at the two smallest sphere sizes, at a matched noise operating point. In a cohort of 45 lesions across nine post-\gls{sirt} patients, \wdtnv{} gave significantly higher lesion \gls{tbr} than all three comparators at approximately matched background \gls{cov}. The simulation and reconstruction software developed to support this work is documented in Appendix~\ref{app:software} and released so that the workflows can be reproduced.

The practical conclusion is narrower than a general claim for multimodal reconstruction. Anatomical information is safest when it steers the geometry of the penalty rather than constraining image intensity or the space of possible solutions. In this count regime, functional coupling and anatomical guidance worked best together, and a prior strong enough to deliver that benefit needed an optimiser that accounted for its curvature. The clinical case remains open: the evaluation was single-centre, used image-quality endpoints rather than absorbed dose, and did not establish robustness to CT artefact or residual registration error. Those are the next tests for the framework.
