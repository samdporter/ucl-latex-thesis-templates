\chapter{Anatomy-informed deconvolution for \gls{pet} partial volume correction}
\label{chapter:deconvolution_pvc}

\section{Introduction and relationship to the thesis}
\label{sec:deconv_intro}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction} established the penalised-likelihood framework for emission tomography, reviewed representative methods for injecting prior knowledge into the optimisation problem (including anatomically guided and kernelised models), and motivated optimisation strategies that remain stable under regularisation. The core of this thesis concerns regularised and synergistic reconstruction under the full Poisson forward model $\bar y(x)=Ax+b$, where ill-posedness and the low-count regime make prior design, preconditioning, and choice of optimisation algorithm critical.

In this chapter we consider a deliberately simplified, image-domain inverse problem: post-reconstruction \acrfull{pvc} via deconvolution with an estimated \acrfull{psf}. Although this is not a substitute for incorporating resolution modelling within reconstruction, it isolates the effects of anatomical guidance and regularisation from subset noise, projector modelling error, and complex system geometry. The chapter serves two main purposes within the broader narrative:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item it connects the priors reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction} (kernel methods, directional regularisation) to a tractable inverse problem where their qualitative behaviour is easily interpreted;
    \item it provides a practical \glsentryshort{pvc} baseline that is straightforward to deploy on retrospectively reconstructed images.
\end{enumerate}

\acrfull{rl} deconvolution is a purely image-domain analogue of \glsentryshort{mlem} under the Poisson model $\bar y(x)=Cx+b$: it is the same \glsentryshort{em} update, with the system matrix $A$ replaced by the \glsentryshort{psf} convolution operator $C$ \cite{richardson_bayesian-based_1972,lucy_iterative_1974,sheppMaximumLikelihoodReconstruction1982,sawatzkyEMTVMethodsInverse2013}. In this setting the ``sensitivity image'' simplifies substantially, since for a unit-sum \glsentryshort{psf} we have $C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}\approx \mathbf{1}$\footnote{$C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}$ everywhere except at the image boundary.}, so the usual \glsentryshort{mlem} normalisation by $A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}$ becomes approximately trivial. We extend \gls{rl} to a penalised deconvolution regime by incorporating a directional total variation prior (Equation~\ref{eq:dtv_defn}), and we construct kernelised deconvolution updates by substituting the kernel image model $x=K\alpha$, exactly as in \gls{kem}/\gls{hkem}. The resulting anatomy-aware deconvolution variants are referred to throughout as \gls{krl}, \gls{hkrl}, and \gls{rldtv}. The following sections give only what differs from Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction}.

\section{Image-domain inverse problem and objective}
\label{sec:deconv_model}

Let $x\in\mathbb{R}^J_+$ denote the unknown (sharper) \gls{pet} activity image on a voxel grid, and let $y\in\mathbb{R}^J_+$ denote an observed blurred image. Although reconstructed \gls{pet} images are not independent raw count data, their local noise is often treated as approximately Poisson in post-reconstruction restoration models \cite{sawatzkyEMTVMethodsInverse2013}. We therefore use the following image-domain approximation:
\begin{equation}
    y_j \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\bar y_j(x)),
    \qquad
    \bar y(x)=Cx+b,
    \qquad j=1,\dots,J,
    \label{eq:deconv_forward_model}
\end{equation}
where $C\in\mathbb{R}_+^{J\times J}$ is a discretised convolution operator representing a space-invariant \glsentryshort{psf}, and $b\in\mathbb{R}^J_+$ is an optional additive background term (set to $0$ in the experiments unless stated otherwise). The operator $C$ plays the same role as the system matrix $A$ in \eqref{eq:forward_model}, but in the image domain; its adjoint $C^{\mathsf T}$ is convolution with the flipped \glsentryshort{psf}. Assuming independence across voxels, the negative log-likelihood $\mathcal D$ and the penalised objective are those of Section~\ref{sec:recon_basics} with $A$ replaced by $C$.

Let $u$ denote a co-registered anatomical guidance image (\gls{mr} or \gls{ct}), resampled to the \gls{pet} grid. It defines either the kernel matrix (\gls{krl}/\gls{hkrl}) or the \gls{dtv} direction field (\gls{rldtv}).

\section{Baseline: Richardson--Lucy deconvolution}
\label{sec:deconv_rl}

\gls{rl} is \glsentryshort{mlem} (Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}) applied to \eqref{eq:deconv_forward_model} with $b=0$ \cite{sheppMaximumLikelihoodReconstruction1982}. Since $C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}\approx\mathbf{1}$, the sensitivity normalisation drops out; for ease of reading, the resulting update is
\begin{equation}
    x^{(k+1)}
    =
    x^{(k)} \odot
        C^{\mathsf T}\!\left( y \oslash (C x^{(k)}) \right).
    \label{eq:rl_update}
\end{equation}
In the scaled-gradient form of Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}, this is a unit step with the \glsentryshort{em} preconditioner $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{(k)}=\operatorname{diag}(x^{(k)})$. As with \glsentryshort{mlem}, unregularised \gls{rl} amplifies noise as iterations increase and relies on early stopping.

\section{Kernelised Richardson--Lucy deconvolution}
\label{sec:deconv_krl}

\gls{krl} applies the kernel image model $x=K\alpha$ of \eqref{eq:kernel1} to deconvolution, with $K$ the sparse, row-normalised kernel matrix built from features of $u$ as in Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction} \cite{wangPETImageReconstruction2015,hutchcroftAnatomicallyaidedPETReconstruction2016}. The forward model becomes $\bar y(\alpha)=CK\alpha+b$, and the corresponding \glsentryshort{em} update, again given for ease of reading, is
\begin{equation}
    \alpha^{(k+1)}
    =
    \alpha^{(k)} \odot
    \frac{
        K^{\mathsf T}C^{\mathsf T}\!\left(
            y \oslash (C K \alpha^{(k)} + b)
        \right)
    }{
        K^{\mathsf T}C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}
    },
    \qquad
    x^{(k+1)} = K\alpha^{(k+1)}.
    \label{eq:krl_update}
\end{equation}
The denominator can be precomputed once, and the update preserves non-negativity given $\alpha^{(0)}\ge 0$. Under the unit-sensitivity approximation the denominator reduces to $K^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}$, which, unlike $C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}$, is generally not close to $\mathbf{1}$ and cannot be ignored.

\gls{hkrl} replaces $K$ with the \gls{hkem} hybrid kernel of Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction}, which adds features from the evolving \gls{pet} estimate to reduce suppression of \gls{pet}-unique structures \cite{deiddaHybridPETMR2019}. Because iteration-dependent kernels can feed noise back into the coupling at late iterations, we freeze the kernel after 5 iterations; for the sphere phantom this proved unnecessary, as the best iteration was usually reached earlier.

\section{Directional-TV regularised RL deconvolution}
\label{sec:deconv_dtv}

\gls{rldtv} uses the \gls{dtv} prior \eqref{eq:dtv_defn} \cite{ehrhardtPETReconstructionAnatomical2016} as an explicit penalty, with the direction field \eqref{eq:xi} computed from $u$. The penalised deconvolution objective is
\begin{equation}
    \hat x
    \in
    \arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^J_+}
    \left\{
        \mathcal{D}(x) + \beta \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{dTV}}(x;u)
    \right\},
    \label{eq:deconv_map_dtv}
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{dTV}}$ is smoothed in the same way as $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{TV}}$ in Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction}. A natural baseline solver adds the prior gradient to the \glsentryshort{em}-preconditioned step of Section~\ref{sec:deconv_rl},
\begin{equation}
    x^{(k+1)}
    =
    \left\{
        x^{(k)}\odot\left(\mathbf{1}
        -
        \varsigma^{(k)}
        \left[
            \nabla_x\mathcal{D}(x^{(k)}) + \beta\,\nabla_x \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{dTV}}(x^{(k)};u)
        \right]\right)
    \right\}_{\geq 0},
    \label{eq:rldtv_update}
\end{equation}
where $\{\}_{\geq0}$ denotes projection onto non-negativity.

Because \eqref{eq:deconv_map_dtv} is smooth with simple bound constraints, the experiments instead optimise it with L-BFGS-B (Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}). This replaces the hand-tuned step sizes $\{\varsigma^{(k)}\}$ with a line search and curvature-informed scaling, and typically reaches a stationary point in far fewer iterations than \eqref{eq:rldtv_update}.

For \gls{rl} and \gls{krl}/\gls{hkrl}, early stopping is essential and is treated as part of the regularisation. In experiments we report results across iteration counts and select a stopping point based on \gls{nrmse} (Equation~\ref{eq:nrmse_krl}) for the phantom and qualitative assessment for the brain example. For \gls{rldtv}, iteration sensitivity is reduced, but a stopping point is still selected to avoid unnecessary computation once the relative objective decrease satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac{
        \mathcal{O}(x^{(k)}) - \mathcal{O}(x^{(k+1)})
    }{
        \max\left\{\left|\mathcal{O}(x^{(k)})\right|,
        \left|\mathcal{O}(x^{(k+1)})\right|,1\right\}
    }
    \leq f_{\mathrm{tol}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal O$ is the constrained objective of Section~\ref{sec:recon_basics} with $\mathcal R=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{dTV}}$; the iterates are feasible, so it equals the finite data-plus-prior value. We use SciPy's default $f_{\mathrm{tol}}=10^{7}\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\approx2.2\times10^{-9}$ (\texttt{ftol}, equivalently $\texttt{factr}=f_{\mathrm{tol}}/\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}=10^{7}$).

\section{Data and experimental setup} \label{sec:deconv_data}
Two datasets are used to characterise behaviour under controlled and realistic conditions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spherical phantom:} a hot-sphere phantom enabling assessment of edge recovery and spill-over (see Figure~\ref{fig:spheres_overview}).

    \item \textbf{$\tau$-\gls{pet} brain with \gls{mri} guidance:} a representative in vivo dataset (see Figure~\ref{fig:brain_overview}).
\end{enumerate}

To obtain a controlled test case with known truth while retaining a realistic resolution/noise regime, the sphere dataset is simulated from a digital activity distribution by forward projecting with an isotropic Gaussian resolution model of $\mathrm{FWHM}=\SI{6}{\milli\metre}$ embedded in the forward projector, adding Poisson noise to the resulting sinograms, and then reconstructing using an operator without additional blurring. This deliberately introduces a resolution-modelling mismatch between simulation and reconstruction. The deconvolution operator used in the subsequent image-domain correction was the same $\SI{6}{\milli\metre}$ Gaussian model, implemented with the same boundary handling as the simulation blur.

Let $x^\star$ denote the digital ground-truth activity image and $\hat{x}$ the reconstructed image. For each sphere $v$, let $\mathcal{S}_v$ be the set of voxel indices belonging to that sphere, and let $\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_v \mathcal{S}_v$ denote the union over all spheres. We report an aggregate \gls{rc} computed by pooling all sphere voxels,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RC} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{S}}\hat{x}_j}{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{S}}x^\star_j}.
\end{equation}

To quantify background noise, let $\mathcal{B}$ denote the set of voxel indices in a warm background region. We compute the spatial background \gls{cov} within $\mathcal{B}$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CoV}_{\mathcal{B}} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar x_{\mathcal{B}}},
\qquad
\bar x_{\mathcal{B}} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{B}}\hat{x}_j,
\qquad
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{B}}\left(\hat{x}_j-\bar x_{\mathcal{B}}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}

This CoV measures variation between voxels within one reconstructed image and is reported as a dimensionless ratio.

Finally, overall fidelity to the truth is summarised by the \gls{nrmse} over the full image grid $\Omega$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nrmse_krl}
\mathrm{NRMSE} \;=\; 
\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{j\in \Omega}\left(\hat{x}_j-x^\star_j\right)^2}}
{\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{j\in \Omega}\left(x^\star_j\right)^2}}.
\end{equation}

The in vivo dataset is a $\tau$-\gls{pet} scan of a healthy volunteer, acquired on a Siemens Biograph Vision 600 using the tracer \textsuperscript{18}F-MK-6240\footnote{Data courtesy of Matthias Koepp, University College London.}, with a co-registered anatomical \glsentryshort{mri} serving as guidance. The \gls{pet} image was reconstructed with \gls{psf}- and \gls{tof}-modelled \gls{osem} (8 iterations, 5 subsets, no post-filter). This dataset is used to qualitatively illustrate how the different priors behave in the presence of heterogeneous uptake patterns and fine anatomical structure, where over-regularisation can lead to anatomical ``imprinting''. The FWHM of the deconvolution operator, $(\SI{4.6}{\milli\metre},\SI{4.6}{\milli\metre},\SI{6.4}{\milli\metre})$, was estimated from a Hoffman brain phantom scan on the same scanner and reconstruction protocol, as the Gaussian smoothing of a co-registered digital Hoffman template that best matched the reconstruction.

\subsection{Hyperparameter tuning}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}

Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search.

For \gls{dtv} reconstructions, we swept the regularisation weight
\[
\beta\in\{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0\}.
\]

Kernel methods used a fixed number of neighbours ($48$ nearest neighbours) within a cubic $9\times9\times9$ neighbourhood \cite{hutchcroftAnatomicallyaidedPETReconstruction2016}. Feature channels were normalised before computing Gaussian similarities, as in the kernel construction described in Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction}; the reported $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_f$ values are therefore interpreted in these normalised feature units. For \gls{krl} we swept the anatomical weighting $\sigma_a$. For \gls{hkrl} we additionally swept the functional weighting $\sigma_f$. Because we used a $k$-nearest-neighbours strategy, distance weights ($\sigma_{df}, \sigma_{da}$) were not used. The implementation of the deconvolution methods used in these experiments is described in Appendix~\ref{app:kernel_rl}.

The requirement for early stopping in \gls{rl}, \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} means that the initialising image itself acts as part of the regularisation. We investigate the effect of initialisation on image quality by running identical sweeps over the sphere dataset initialising with a uniform image of ones and the blurred \gls{pet} image.

$\tau$-\gls{pet} brain data reconstructions were initialised using a uniform image of ones for \gls{rl}/\gls{krl}/\gls{hkrl} and the blurred \gls{pet} image for \gls{rldtv}.

Across both initialisation strategies, \gls{rldtv} achieved the lowest \gls{nrmse} (0.0177), the highest aggregate \gls{rc} (0.889), and the lowest background \gls{cov} (0.123). \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} substantially improved on unregularised \gls{rl}, but were almost indistinguishable from one another in this simple sphere phantom. With blurred initialisation, the best \gls{rl} result occurred at iteration 0, indicating that unconstrained \gls{rl} deconvolution immediately increased the \gls{nrmse} once applied to this noisy reconstruction.

Figure~\ref{fig:deconv_rc_cov_selected} shows the recovery--noise trajectories for the selected hyperparameter setting of each method. The trajectories and their selected operating points change with the initial image for \gls{rl}, \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl}, demonstrating that their reported performance depends on both initialisation and early stopping. For \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl}, the iterates are updated in kernel-coefficient space, whereas recovery and background \gls{cov} are evaluated after mapping the coefficients to the image domain. Under uniform initialisation, the coefficient image is normalised from the uniform image before this mapping; consequently, the apparent starting positions of the kernelised trajectories are a parametrisation effect rather than a recovery--noise advantage. The same convention is used for the full hybrid-kernel reconstruction in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}. Selection by minimum \gls{nrmse} is possible in this controlled phantom because the ground truth is known, but an equivalent stopping rule is not directly available for clinical data. In contrast, \gls{rldtv} reaches the same selected recovery, background \gls{cov} and \gls{nrmse} from both initialisations, although the number of optimiser iterations differs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deconv_discussion}

Deblurring is inherently ill-posed: the \gls{psf} attenuates high frequencies, so naive unregularised \gls{rl} deconvolution magnifies noise. Anatomical guidance provides a structured way to preferentially preserve edges that are plausible given $u$ while damping noise-like oscillations, either by restricting the solution space (kernel methods) or by penalising anatomically inconsistent gradients (\gls{dtv}).

All guidance-based methods risk bias when the guidance image does not reflect tracer uptake boundaries. With non-negative coefficients, \gls{krl} restricts the image to the cone $K\mathbb{R}_+^J=\{K\alpha:\alpha\ge0\}$, generally smaller than the linear space $\mathrm{range}(K)$; \gls{rldtv} is less restrictive but can still encourage over-alignment where the data term is weak (low counts, low contrast). \gls{hkrl} partially mitigates mismatch by allowing the coupling structure to adapt to \gls{pet} features, but must be controlled (e.g.\ by freezing) to avoid noise-driven feedback.

The full reconstruction problem has the same failure mode: guidance must not suppress information present in only one modality. This constraint motivates the synergistic prior developed in the following chapters.

The present study should be viewed as a proof-of-principle rather than a definitive validation. A stronger evaluation would require a larger set of patient scans and more demanding controlled simulations, for example BrainWeb-style digital brain phantoms with inserted \gls{pet}-unique lesions or tumours. Such work would allow the qualitative observations here to be tested with population-level variability and known ground truth.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:deconv_summary}

This chapter formulated post-reconstruction \gls{pet} \gls{pvc} as an approximate image-domain Poisson inverse problem $\bar y(x)=Cx+b$ and studied \gls{rl} deconvolution and three anatomy-aware regularised variants. \gls{krl} and \gls{hkrl} implement implicit regularisation through kernelised image models, consistent with the kernel framework reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction} \cite{wangPETImageReconstruction2015,hutchcroftAnatomicallyaidedPETReconstruction2016,deiddaHybridPETMR2019}. \gls{rldtv} implements explicit anatomically guided regularisation via a \gls{dtv} penalty \cite{ehrhardtPETReconstructionAnatomical2016} using the same directional notation as Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction}. These experiments provide a practical \gls{pvc} baseline and a controlled setting for diagnosing guidance-driven bias and noise amplification. The findings inform the synergistic priors and optimisation methods developed in later chapters.
