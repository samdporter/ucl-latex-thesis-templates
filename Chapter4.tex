\section{Challenges in Bremsstrahlung Imaging}

As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:y90}, unlike diagnostic radionuclides such as \isotope[99m]{Tc} that emit gamma photons at discrete photopeaks, \isotope[90]{Y} has no practically useful prompt gamma emission for SPECT. Imaging therefore relies on detecting the continuous spectrum of Bremsstrahlung photons generated as emitted $\beta$-particles decelerate in tissue \cite{woerner_advances_2022}. This emission physics gives rise to several fundamental challenges for quantitative reconstruction:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous energy spectrum:} The emitted photon energies span a continuum from (near) zero up to the maximum $\beta$-energy of 2.28~MeV \cite{be_table_2004} (see Figure~\ref{fig:brems_spectra}). In conventional SPECT, scatter may be estimated using energy windows adjacent to a photopeak; for \isotope[90]{Y}, the absence of a photopeak makes it difficult to separate primary photons from those that have undergone Compton scatter within the patient. As a result, more sophisticated scatter-modelling and correction strategies are typically required \cite{rong_development_2012}.
    
    \item \textbf{Septal penetration and collimator design:} The high-energy tail of the Bremsstrahlung spectrum increases the probability of septal penetration, which degrades spatial resolution and contrast. Collimator choice and optimisation are therefore critical to limit penetration while maintaining adequate sensitivity \cite{rong_collimator_2013}.
    
    \item \textbf{Bremsstrahlung-induced blur:} Bremsstrahlung photons are produced along the electron track rather than at a single point, so the effective photon emission location is distributed relative to the \isotope[90]{Y} decay site. This introduces an additional range-dependent blurring component that is not present in photopeak imaging and can further degrade reconstructed resolution \cite{rong_development_2012} (see Figure.
\end{itemize}

To overcome these limitations, optimisation must occur at both the acquisition and reconstruction stages. While hardware choices, such as the preference for Medium Energy General Purpose (MEGP) or High Energy General Purpose (HEGP) collimators, can mitigate septal penetration \cite{taherparvar_evaluation_2019, kim_effects_2019, ince_collimator_2021}, substantial gains in quantitative accuracy are achieved through advanced computational modeling \cite{dewaraja_improved_2017}.

\gls{mc} simulation codes, such as SIMIND \cite{ljungberg_-_2012} and \gls{gate} \cite{jan_gate_2004, rault_fast_2010}, play a pivotal role in this optimisation. These tools allow for the generation of accurate scatter kernels, as well as other effects that are specific to the continuous \isotope[90]{Y} spectrum. Some methods have integrated modelling of the spatial offset between the $\beta$-decay location and the point of Bremsstrahlung emission into \glsentryshort{mc} forward projectors to refine the system matrix \cite{rong_development_2012}.

\section{Additive Corrections}
\label{sec:additive_corrections}

A key difficulty in quantitative \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung SPECT is that a substantial fraction of the measured counts arise from photons that are not well modelled by a purely ``primary-only'' forward projector. In this work we treat these contributions as \emph{additive terms} in the Poisson measurement model, with a particular focus on (patient) scatter.

\subsection{Statistical model with an additive background term}

Let $y \in \mathbb{N}^{I}$ denote the measured projection data (all bins across all views) and let $x \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{J}$ denote the unknown activity image. We use the standard Poisson model
\begin{equation}
y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left( (A x)_i + b_i \right), \qquad i = 1,\dots,I,
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the system matrix modelling the (primary) detection process (including attenuation and detector response as implemented in our reconstruction pipeline), and $b \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}$ is an \emph{additive} component. In Bremsstrahlung SPECT, $b$ is typically dominated by Compton-scattered photons, with additional contributions possible from other backgrounds (e.g.\ stray radiation or modelling mismatch). The role of additive correction is therefore to provide an estimate of $b$ that is consistent with the acquisition protocol and reconstruction model.

Given a \emph{fixed} $b$, \glsentryshort{osem} yields an approximate maximum-likelihood estimate of $x$ under this Poisson model. However, in \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung imaging, $b$ is large and impossible to estimate from neighbouring energy-window , motivating model-based strategies.

\subsection{Initial scatter estimate via a pre-trained deep network}

To initialise $b$ we use a deep-learning scatter estimator that predicts scatter projections directly from the measured emission projections (and \glsentryshort{ct}-derived attenuation information), following the  approach by Xiang \textem{et al.}~ who trained a convolutional neural network to regress \glsentryshort{mc}-derived ``true'' scatter \cite{xiang_deep_2020}.

In our case this initialisation is explicitly heuristic: the available pre-trained network was developed and trained under a different hardware/protocol configuration (scanner, collimation, energy windowing, and number of views) than the Siemens and Mediso datasets used in this thesis \cite{xiang_deep_2020}. In particular, the network was trained on data acquired on a Symbia Intevo SPECT/CT system with high-energy collimation and a 105--195~keV window with 80 projections, whereas our reconstructions were performed on Siemens or Mediso systems with different energy windows and either MEGP (Siemens) or MLEGP (Mediso) collimation. Despite this domain shift, we found that the predicted scatter term fits the measured data sufficiently well to provide a sensible starting point for subsequent model-based refinement (Figure \ref{fig:nema_scatter_est}). 

\subsection{Iterative OSEM--SIMIND refinement loop}

Starting from the deep-learning initialisation $b^{(0)}$, we then refine the additive term using an alternating procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction step:} update $x$ using OSEM and the current additive estimate $b^{(k)}$. We used 12 subsets and 10 epochs (total 120 subiterations), warm-started from the previous outer iterate.
    \item \textbf{Regularisation of the intermediate image:} apply an isotropic Gaussian post-smoothing of 5~mm \gls{fwhm} to the intermediate reconstruction (used only for the subsequent scatter simulation step).
    \item \textbf{Scatter simulation step:} run a SIMIND-based Monte Carlo scatter simulation using the (smoothed) current activity estimate to produce a new scatter estimate $\widehat{b}^{(k+1)}$.
\end{enumerate}
We repeat this OSEM $\rightarrow$ SIMIND $\rightarrow$ OSEM$+$5~mm smoothing $\rightarrow \dots$ loop for 10 outer iterations. The 5~mm smoothing is used to suppress high-frequency noise that would otherwise be amplified by repeatedly re-simulating scatter from noisy intermediate reconstructions.

\subsection{Offline versus online additive correction strategies}

We consider two practical strategies for how the scatter term is used throughout the full reconstruction:

\paragraph{Offline (fixed) additive correction.}
In the \emph{offline} strategy, we compute a scatter estimate once (after the initialisation/refinement loop) and then keep it fixed for the remainder of the reconstruction. Concretely, we set $b \leftarrow b^{(\star)}$ and run OSEM to convergence (or for a fixed number of iterations) with a constant additive term. This approach is typically stable, but may be biased if the fixed scatter estimate is inconsistent with the final activity estimate.

\paragraph{Online (intermittently updated) additive correction.}
In the \emph{online} strategy, we update the scatter estimate during reconstruction. That is, after some number of OSEM iterations, we re-run the SIMIND scatter simulation using the current image estimate and update $b$. This is conceptually aligned with hybrid Monte-Carlo/analytical reconstruction approaches in which the scatter estimate is updated a small number of times until it stabilises \cite{dewaraja_improved_2017,elschot_quantitative_2013}. In practice, the update cadence is a tunable choice: too frequent updates increase computational cost and can introduce instability; too infrequent updates reduce the benefit relative to the offline approach.

\subsection{Full replacement versus damped scatter updates}

For the online strategy, we investigate two update rules.

\paragraph{(1) Full replacement.}
We set
\begin{equation}
b^{(k+1)} \leftarrow \widehat{b}^{(k+1)}.
\end{equation}
Although appealingly direct, we observed that full replacement can be unstable in some cases, with the behaviour varying across phantoms. A practical interpretation is that once $b$ is changed, the likelihood model being optimised by OSEM is also changed; therefore the overall alternating procedure is not guaranteed to behave monotonically. An 

\paragraph{(2) Damped (relaxed) updates.}
To improve stability, we use a convex combination of the old and new scatter estimates:
\begin{equation}
b^{(k+1)} \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\, b^{(k)} + \alpha\, \widehat{b}^{(k+1)},
\qquad \alpha \in (0,1].
\end{equation}
This can be viewed as a relaxation of the fixed-point iteration $b = \widehat{b}(x)$, reducing sensitivity to noise and modelling mismatch at the cost of slower adaptation. In all experiments here we set $\alpha = 0.2$. This value was chosen heuristically; we did not perform a dedicated study to optimise $\alpha$, but empirically it provided a good stability--convergence trade-off across datasets.

\medskip
Overall, these offline/online and replacement/damping variants provide a practical framework for controlling the bias--variance and stability properties of Bremsstrahlung additive correction, while keeping the reconstruction pipeline compatible with Monte Carlo scatter modelling.

\section{Bremsstrahlung Range Blur}
\label{sec:brem_range_blur}

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/Chapter3/Gaussian_bremss_range.png}
    \caption{Example fits of an isotropic Gaussian proxy to the Monte Carlo source-to-emission displacement distribution for \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung in water (clinical energy windows).}
    \label{fig:gaussian_bremss_range}
\end{figure}

As mentioned previously, bremsstrahlung photons are generated along the $\beta$-particle track, inducing an additional \emph{range blur} that is absent in photopeak imaging. Several \glsentryshort{mc} frameworks can model this effect using detailed, energy-dependent \glspl{pdf} for the displacement between the decay location and the photon emission location. However, in practice, most general-purpose reconstruction toolkits are not designed to ingest arbitrary, precomputed displacement \glspl{pdf} within the forward projector and its adjoint.

Here we adopt a pragmatic approximation: for clinically relevant energy windows and in water-equivalent tissue, the marginal displacement distribution is observed to be close to Gaussian. We therefore model the Bremsstrahlung range blur as an \emph{isotropic image-space Gaussian} whose variance is selected to match \glsentryshort{mc}-derived displacement statistics (Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_bremss_range}). This is simple to implement (a single 3D Gaussian smoothing) and is compatible with standard projector architectures.

\subsection{Projector point-spread function}
\label{subsec:projector_psf}

We model the collimator--detector response (CDR) by a  Gaussian \gls{psf} whose width increases with the source--collimator distance $h$ and depends on energy $E$. In the projector we represent the depth-dependent blur by a spatially variant isotropic Gaussian with standard deviation
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}(h;E) = c_0(E) + c_1(E)\, h.
\label{eq:sigma_lin_def}
\end{equation}
This linear model approximates the standard quadrature combination of intrinsic detector resolution and geometric collimator resolution,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}(h;E)
= \bigl[\;\sigma_{\mathrm{intr}}(E)^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{geom}}(h; E)^2\;\bigr]^{1/2},
\label{eq:sigma_true}
\end{equation}
while avoiding repeated evaluation of a square root inside the projector. The coefficients $c_0(E)$ and $c_1(E)$ are obtained by a least-squares fit of $\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}(h_i;E)$ to $\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}(h_i;E)$ over a clinically relevant distance range ($h \in [2,20]\,\mathrm{cm}$). Writing
\begin{equation}
\bar h = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N h_i,
\qquad
\bar\sigma = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_{\mathrm{true}}(h_i;E),
\end{equation}
the closed-form solution (assuming $\sum_i(h_i-\bar h)^2>0$) is
\begin{equation}
c_1(E)
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (h_i-\bar h)\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}(h_i;E)-\bar\sigma)}
     {\sum_{i=1}^N (h_i-\bar h)^2},
\qquad
c_0(E)
=
\bar\sigma - c_1(E)\,\bar h .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Intrinsic detector spatial response.}
Intrinsic resolution is measured using a standard source \cite{pells_quantitative_2023} and modelled as an isotropic Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSF}_{\mathrm{intr}}(\mathbf r;E)=
\mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf r;\ \mathbf 0,\ \sigma_{\mathrm{intr}}^2(E)\,\mathbf I\right).
\label{eq:intrinsic}
\end{equation}
When intrinsic resolution is reported as $\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{intr}}(E)$, we convert using
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{intr}}(E) = \frac{\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{intr}}(E)}{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Geometric collimator response with penetration via effective length.}
For a parallel-hole collimator, the (FWHM) geometric resolution at distance $h$ is commonly approximated by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{geom}}(h;E)
\approx d_{\mathrm{eff}}
\left(1+\frac{h}{l_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)}\right),
\label{eq:fwhm_geom}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective hole diameter and $l_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)$ is an \emph{effective} hole length used to account for septal penetration. We use
\begin{equation}
l_{\mathrm{eff}}(E) \;=\; l \;-\; \frac{2}{\mu_{\mathrm{Pb}}(E)} ,
\qquad
\mu_{\mathrm{Pb}}(E)\ \text{interpolated from NIST XCOM tables},
\label{eq:leff}
\end{equation}
where $l$ is the physical hole length and $\mu_{\mathrm{Pb}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of lead at energy $E$ \cite{noauthor_xcom_2009}. Converting from FWHM to standard deviation gives
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{geom}}(h;E)=\frac{\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{geom}}(h;E)}{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}}.
\label{eq:sigma_geom}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Bremsstrahlung-specific blur}
\label{subsec:brem_specific_blur}

\paragraph{Gaussian proxy for range blur.}
Let $\Delta$ denote the displacement between the \isotope[90]{Y} decay location and the Bremsstrahlung photon emission location. Rather than using a detailed displacement PDF inside the projector, we approximate the induced range blur by an isotropic Gaussian kernel
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSF}_{\mathrm{brem}}(\mathbf r;E)
=\mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf r;\ \mathbf 0,\ \sigma_{\mathrm{brem}}^2(E)\,\mathbf I\right).
\label{eq:brem}
\end{equation}
For each clinical energy window, $\sigma_{\mathrm{brem}}(E)$ is obtained from a coarse \gls{lut} by fitting a zero-mean Gaussian proxy to \glsentryshort{mc} displacement curves for \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung in water from \glsentryshort{gate} fast-simulation studies \cite{rault_fast_2010} (Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_bremss_range}).

\paragraph{Composite energy windows.}
For a set of energy windows $\mathcal S$ combined into a single projection dataset, we collapse the corresponding Gaussian mixture by second-moment matching:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{clin}}^2
=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} w_i\,\sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} w_i},
\qquad
w_i \propto \text{per-window counts}.
\label{eq:moment_match}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Implementation in image space.}
Because the convolution of isotropic Gaussians is Gaussian, incorporating Bremsstrahlung range blur is equivalent to adding variances. In practice we implement this as an image-space convolution by $\mathrm{PSF}_{\mathrm{brem}}$ (i.e.\ a 3D Gaussian smoothing) applied to the current activity estimate before forward projection. This keeps the forward model compatible with standard projector implementations while capturing the dominant range-broadening effect in water-equivalent tissue.

\subsection{Validation against SIMIND and GATE forward projections}
\label{subsec:brem_blur_validation}

We assessed how well the isotropic Gaussian proxy reproduces the broader displacement PDFs (including angular effects in GATE) by comparing projected point-source responses produced by:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item STIR forward projections with geometric CDR modelling only;
    \item STIR forward projections with geometric CDR plus the proposed isotropic Bremsstrahlung range blur;
    \item SIMIND simulations (which use an approximate isotropic Bremsstrahlung emission model); and
    \item GATE simulations (used here as a higher-fidelity reference that includes angular dependence of Bremsstrahlung emission).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Point-source-in-sphere configuration.}
A true \isotope[90]{Y} point source is not physically realisable at clinical activity levels because the $\beta$-particles have a finite range. To approximate a point source for spatial response measurements, we embedded the activity within a small water-equivalent sphere of radius $1.2\,\mathrm{cm}$, consistent with the maximum $\beta$-particle range in soft tissue/water (approximately $11\,\mathrm{mm}$) \cite{pasciak_radioembolization_2014}. For each configuration we measured the detector-plane FWHM at source--collimator distances of $2$, $5$, $10$, $15$, and $20\,\mathrm{cm}$.

\paragraph{Clinical energy windows.}
Comparisons were performed for three representative clinical windows:
\begin{equation}
[38.24,126.16]\,\mathrm{keV},
\qquad
[50.0,150.0]\,\mathrm{keV},
\qquad
[75.0,225.0]\,\mathrm{keV}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
This validation focuses on water-equivalent media, which is appropriate for the liver (the primary application in post-radioembolization imaging). Differences in range and scatter properties in lung or cortical bone are not explicitly modelled here and are not expected to dominate performance for liver-focused dosimetry.

\section{Residual Correction}
\label{sec:residual_correction}

Fu and Qi proposed a residual-correction (RC) strategy to accelerate reconstructions when an \emph{accurate} forward model is too expensive to apply at every iteration \cite{fu_residual_2010}. In their positron-range setting, the accurate system model can be expressed as a fast projector composed with an additional blurring operator. RC then intermittently estimates the modelling discrepancy and folds it into an additive term, allowing subsequent iterations to proceed using the computationally cheaper projector.

In this work we investigate whether RC can be applied analogously to \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung SPECT reconstruction. Empirically, we find that RC behaves well for high-count, low-background phantom data, but can become unreliable in low-count clinical-like regimes where regularisation is necessarily strong. We attribute this behaviour to the fact that RC corrects the \emph{forward prediction} but does not, in general, restore a \emph{matched} forward/adjoint pair inside a regularised reconstruction.

\subsection{Residual correction update}
\label{subsec:rc_update}

Let $A_{\star}$ denote the fast (computationally cheap) system matrix used in the iterative updates and let $A_{\mathrm{MC}}$ denote a more accurate (expensive) system model (here, Monte Carlo based). We consider the Poisson model
\begin{equation}
y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left( (A_{\star}x)_i + b_{E,i} \right),
\end{equation}
where $b_E$ is an additive correction term (e.g.\ scatter). At spaced iterations, we estimate the discrepancy between the accurate and fast forward predictions at the current iterate by approximately solving
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} \approx \underset{x\geq0}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(x \mid y\;; b_E, A_{\star}\right),
\end{equation}
and then forming the residual
\begin{equation}
(\Delta A)\hat{x} \;=\; A_{\mathrm{MC}}\hat{x} - A_{\star}\hat{x}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
This residual is incorporated into the additive term,
\begin{equation}
b_E^{(\mathrm{new})} \;=\; b_E^{(\mathrm{old})} + (\Delta A)\hat{x},
\label{eq:be_update}
\end{equation}
after which reconstruction proceeds again under the updated additive correction. In favourable regimes, this produces forward predictions closer to those of $A_{\mathrm{MC}}$ while retaining the per-iteration cost of $A_{\star}$.

\subsection{Regularised objective with a Relative Difference prior}
\label{subsec:rc_objective_rdp}

In Bremsstrahlung SPECT, low-count data frequently necessitate comparatively strong regularisation. In all RC experiments reported here, we reconstruct using a Relative Difference prior (RDP), i.e.\ we solve a MAP problem of the form
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}
=
\underset{x\ge 0}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}
\left\{
\Phi_{A_{\star}}(x)
+
\beta R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)
\right\},
\label{eq:map_rdp}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{A_{\star}}(x)$ is the negative Poisson log-likelihood (up to constants),
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{A_{\star}}(x)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{I}
\left[
(A_{\star}x)_i + b_{E,i}
-
y_i \log\!\bigl((A_{\star}x)_i + b_{E,i}\bigr)
\right],
\label{eq:poisson_nll}
\end{equation}
and the Relative Difference prior is defined over a neighbourhood system $\mathcal{N}$ by
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)
=
\sum_{(j,k)\in \mathcal{N}}
w_{jk}\;
\frac{(x_j-x_k)^2}{(x_j+x_k)^2+\eta^2}.
\label{eq:rdp_prior}
\end{equation}
Here, $x_j$ and $x_k$ denote neighbouring voxels, $w_{jk}\ge 0$ are neighbourhood weights, and $\eta>0$ is a small smoothing constant that prevents numerical instability when $x_j+x_k$ is small. The hyperparameter $\beta>0$ controls the overall strength of regularisation.

\subsection{Interpretation via matched versus unmatched projectors}
\label{subsec:rc_unmatched}

To understand why RC can be fragile in low-count, strongly regularised settings, it is helpful to view the method through the lens of \emph{matched} versus \emph{unmatched} projector pairs.

\paragraph{Set-up.}
Suppose the accurate forward model can be written as a fast projector composed with an additional blur (or resolution) operator $K$,
\begin{equation}
P := A_{\mathrm{fast}}K,
\qquad
\bar y(x) = Px + r = A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx + r,
\end{equation}
with $r$ an additive background term and Poisson data $y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\bar y(x))$.

\paragraph{Maximum likelihood (no regularisation).}
The negative log-likelihood for forward model $P$ has gradient
\begin{equation}
\nabla \Phi_{P}(x) = P^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{Px+r}\right).
\end{equation}
For $P=A_{\mathrm{fast}}K$, the \emph{matched} stationarity condition is
\begin{equation}
K^T A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx+r}\right)=0.
\label{eq:matched_ml}
\end{equation}
If instead one uses the fast backprojector $A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T$ with the same forward model (an \emph{unmatched} pair), the stationarity condition would be
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx+r}\right)=0.
\label{eq:unmatched_ml}
\end{equation}
In the special case where $K^T$ is injective (and numerically well-conditioned on the relevant subspace), \eqref{eq:matched_ml} and \eqref{eq:unmatched_ml} share the same stationary points, since $K^T z=0 \Rightarrow z=0$. This explains why unmatched backprojection can appear benign in some unregularised, high-count regimes.

\paragraph{How RC fits.}
Residual correction aims to make the \emph{forward prediction} behave like the accurate model without applying $K$ at every iteration, i.e.
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx \;\approx\; A_{\mathrm{fast}}x + \mathrm{RC}(x),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RC}(x)$ approximates $(A_{\mathrm{fast}}K-A_{\mathrm{fast}})x$ (often using lagged/intermittent updates). If the correction is consistent at a limit point $x^\star$, then the ratio term $y/(A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx+r)$ is effectively ``accurate-forward''.
However, the backprojection used by the iterative update remains $A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T$, not $(A_{\mathrm{fast}}K)^T = K^T A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T$. In this sense, RC can be interpreted as correcting the forward model while still behaving like an \emph{unmatched} method on the adjoint side.

\paragraph{Why low-count + RDP regularisation is high-risk.}
With an RDP prior, the MAP stationarity condition (ignoring the non-negativity constraint) is
\begin{equation}
P^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{Px+r}\right) + \beta \nabla R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)=0.
\label{eq:map_stationary_general}
\end{equation}
For $P=A_{\mathrm{fast}}K$, the matched condition becomes
\begin{equation}
K^T A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx+r}\right) + \beta \nabla R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)=0,
\label{eq:matched_map}
\end{equation}
whereas an unmatched analogue would be
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{fast}}^T\!\left(1-\frac{y}{A_{\mathrm{fast}}Kx+r}\right) + \beta \nabla R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)=0.
\label{eq:unmatched_map}
\end{equation}
As total counts decrease, the likelihood term becomes flatter and noisier, so the relative influence of $\beta \nabla R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)$ increases even when $\beta$ is held fixed. Unlike the ML case, the factor $K^T$ multiplies only the data-term gradient in \eqref{eq:matched_map} and does not act on $\nabla R_{\mathrm{RDP}}(x)$. Therefore, for $\beta>0$, matched and unmatched fixed points generally differ. In this prior-dominated regime, using an unmatched backprojector while correcting only the forward prediction can substantially alter the balance between data fidelity and regularisation, leading to marked divergence from the solution associated with the fully matched accurate model.

\subsection{Empirical observations}
\label{subsec:rc_empirical}

For high-count, low-background phantom acquisitions (e.g.\ NEMA-style datasets), RC substantially improves agreement with accurate forward projections and remains stable. For low-count Bremsstrahlung acquisitions, however, we observe that RC can become unreliable when combined with strong RDP regularisation. These observations are consistent with the interpretation above: in prior-dominated regimes, mismatched adjoints change the effective weighting of the data term relative to the RDP, and RC does not in general restore a matched forward/adjoint pair.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:brems_eval}

The preceding sections introduced four modelling choices that can be layered onto
the \isotope[90]{Y} Bremsstrahlung forward model: an image-space Gaussian
approximation to the range blur (Section~\ref{sec:brem_range_blur}); an additive
scatter term that is either fixed (\emph{offline}) or updated on-the-fly
(\emph{online}) during reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:additive_corrections});
a damped rather than full update of that additive term
(Section~\ref{subsec:rc_update}); and a Fu \& Qi-style residual correction of the
resolution model (Section~\ref{sec:residual_correction}). This section evaluates
each of these on measured data, and asks not whether any single option is
universally ``best'', but how each behaves as the regularisation strength is
varied and how they interact.

\subsection{Datasets and reconstruction protocol}
\label{subsec:brems_datasets}

We evaluated the corrections on three measured phantom acquisitions and one
patient study, chosen to span the range of background activity encountered in
post-radioembolization imaging:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NEMA (cold background):} a NEMA IEC body phantom with six
    equal-concentration hot spheres ($10$--$37\,\mathrm{mm}$) in a
    (nominally) cold background.
    \item \textbf{NEMA (warm background):} the same phantom geometry with an
    $8{:}1$ sphere-to-background activity ratio. The $17\,\mathrm{mm}$ sphere was
    unfilled and is excluded; the two smallest spheres
    ($\le 13\,\mathrm{mm}$) are noise-limited at these count levels and are
    excluded from the quantitative analysis.
    \item \textbf{Anthropomorphic liver:} an anthropomorphic phantom with a
    single hot lesion insert in a warm liver compartment
    ($\approx 4{:}1$).
    \item \textbf{Patient (SIRT-3):} a clinical post-\gls{sirt} \isotope[90]{Y}
    acquisition with five liver lesions delineated in the co-registered
    \isotope[90]{Y} \gls{pet} space.
\end{itemize}

Each reconstruction was performed with \gls{osem} using $12$ staggered subsets and
$10$ epochs ($120$ subset updates), warm-started from the previous outer iterate.
The prior was included through a one-step-late gradient step applied after each
subset update, using the Relative Difference prior (RDP,
Equation~\ref{eq:rdp_prior}) with weight
$\beta\in\{0,\,10^{-3},\,10^{-2},\,10^{-1}\}$; unless a $\beta$-sweep is shown, all
other results use $\beta=10^{-2}$. The SPECTUB system matrix modelled attenuation
(from the co-registered \gls{ct}) and a depth-dependent Gaussian
collimator--detector response. The Bremsstrahlung range blur, when enabled, was
the isotropic $6.9\,\mathrm{mm}$-\gls{fwhm} image-space Gaussian of
Section~\ref{subsec:brem_specific_blur}, applied to the activity estimate inside
the acquisition model before forward projection. This reconstruction was embedded
in the outer \mbox{OSEM\,$\rightarrow$\,SIMIND} additive-update loop of
Section~\ref{sec:additive_corrections}, run for $10$ outer iterations; between
outer iterations the reconstruction was smoothed with a $5\,\mathrm{mm}$-\gls{fwhm}
isotropic Gaussian before being used as the source for the next Monte Carlo
simulation.

\paragraph{Additive-term construction (reproducibility).}
At each outer iteration $k$, a single SIMIND PENETRATE simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:additive_corrections}) was run on the current, body-masked
reconstruction, yielding two projection components: the total detected fluence
$p_{\mathrm{tot}}^{(k)}$ (all interactions) and the geometrically collimated,
attenuated primary $p_{\mathrm{geo}}^{(k)}$. The Monte Carlo output was calibrated
to the fast \gls{spect} forward projector $A_{\star}$ by a scalar
$s^{(k)}$, computed as a ratio-of-sums of $A_{\star}x^{(k)}$ to
$p_{\mathrm{geo}}^{(k)}$ after trimming the $10\%$ most extreme per-bin ratios for
robustness. The two corrections were then formed as
\begin{align}
\widehat{b}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{(k)} &= s^{(k)}\bigl(p_{\mathrm{tot}}^{(k)}-p_{\mathrm{geo}}^{(k)}\bigr)
&&\text{(scatter: all non-geometric-primary counts),}\\
(\Delta A)x^{(k)} &= s^{(k)}\,p_{\mathrm{geo}}^{(k)} - A_{\star}x^{(k)}
&&\text{(residual, cf.\ Equation~\ref{eq:residual}).}
\end{align}
Each term was folded into the running additive estimate by the damped update of
Section~\ref{subsec:rc_update} --- the scatter term with $\alpha=0.2$, the residual
term with $\alpha\in\{0.2,\,1\}$ --- and the effective additive term applied to the
\gls{osem} step, $b^{(k)}=b_{\mathrm{sc}}^{(k)}+(\Delta A)x^{(k)}$ (with only the
component(s) active for a given strategy), was projected to non-negativity before
each forward projection. The initial additive term $b^{(0)}$ was the pre-trained
deep-learning scatter estimate of Section~\ref{sec:additive_corrections}; for the
fixed-scatter strategies it was held constant. The body mask used to suppress
out-of-patient activity before each simulation was obtained by thresholding the
attenuation map (at $0.05\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$), with a morphological opening
(radius $2$) and retention of the largest connected component. Crossing the two
scatter options (fixed/updated), the residual option (off/on), the range blur
(off/on) and the four values of $\beta$ yields the four correction
\emph{strategies} --- fixed scatter, fixed scatter\,$+$\,residual, updated
scatter, and updated scatter\,$+$\,residual --- each evaluated across the blur and
$\beta$ axes.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo (SIMIND) configuration.}
All simulations used \gls{mc} code SIMIND \cite{ljungberg_-_2012} with the
PENETRATE scoring routine (which resolves geometric collimation, septal
penetration and collimator scatter as separate components) and the
Y-90 Frey Bremsstrahlung source model. Common settings were a
$9.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ \gls{nai} crystal, a $10\,\mathrm{keV}$ energy-bin width up to
$2.08\,\mathrm{MeV}$, a photon-history multiplier of $1$, and a low-energy
photon-history cutoff at $75\%$ of the lower window threshold. The scanner-,
collimator- and window-specific settings, which differ between the Mediso and
Siemens acquisitions, are listed in Table~\ref{tab:brems_simind}.

    \begin{tabular}{lccccc}

        \hline

The regularisation strength $\beta$ is not itself an object of study here: it is
well established that increasing the RDP weight suppresses noise and, beyond a
point, also suppresses contrast recovery. We instead sweep $\beta$ so that each
correction strategy traces out a recovery--noise \emph{operating curve}, allowing
the strategies to be compared fairly at matched noise rather than at a single
arbitrary $\beta$.

\subsection{Figures of merit}
\label{subsec:brems_fom}

No spatially co-registered ground-truth image exists for any of these
acquisitions (the only available \isotope[90]{Y} \gls{pet} volumes are not
registered to the \gls{spect} grid). We therefore avoid any image-to-reference
comparison and report only scale-invariant quantities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recovery coefficient (RC):} the measured activity ratio between
    a hot \gls{voi} and a reference region, divided by the \emph{known} true
    ratio, so that perfect recovery gives $\mathrm{RC}=1$. For the
    \emph{cold-background} NEMA phantom the spheres are all filled to the
    \emph{same} concentration, so the reference is taken to be the eroded
    \emph{centre of the largest ($37\,\mathrm{mm}$) sphere} and each sphere's
    recovery is $\mathrm{RC}=\overline{x}_{\mathrm{sphere}}/\overline{x}_{37\,\mathrm{mm\,centre}}$,
    with an ideal value of $1$ for every sphere (the $37\,\mathrm{mm}$ sphere thus
    measures its own whole-VOI mean against its centre). Recovery is reported for
    the six spheres ($10,13,17,22,28,37\,\mathrm{mm}$). For the
    \emph{warm-background} NEMA phantom the sphere-to-background ratio is normalised
    by the known $8{:}1$ (the unfilled $17\,\mathrm{mm}$ sphere is excluded, and the
    two smallest spheres, $\le13\,\mathrm{mm}$, are noise-limited and excluded from
    the quantitative summaries); for the anthropomorphic phantom the lesion-to-liver
    ratio is normalised by the known $\approx4{:}1$ concentration.
    \item \textbf{Noise:} the voxel-wise coefficient of variation (CoV, standard
    deviation over mean) in a uniform region (the $37\,\mathrm{mm}$-sphere centre
    for cold NEMA; the background compartment for warm NEMA; the liver for the
    anthropomorphic phantom; a background region for the patient).
    \item \textbf{Patient lesion contrast:} as no known activity ratio is
    available \emph{in vivo}, we use the lesion-to-background uptake ratio as a
    contrast proxy.
\end{itemize}
All quantities are read at the converged tenth outer iteration unless a
convergence trajectory is shown explicitly. To keep comparisons fair, the reported
paired effects hold every other factor fixed and change only the correction under
test. Except where recovery is resolved by sphere size
(Figure~\ref{fig:brems_residual_size}), a single reported recovery coefficient is
the mean over the evaluated spheres of the phantom.

\subsection{On-the-fly scatter update: gains, cost and stability}
\label{subsec:res_update}

Updating the additive scatter term during reconstruction, rather than fixing it,
is the most aggressive of the four modelling choices, and its behaviour is
strongly dataset-dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:brems_scatter_dynamics}). On the
warm-background phantom the online update raises recovery substantially
(mean $\Delta\mathrm{RC}\approx+0.10$ relative to a fixed scatter term) but nearly
\emph{doubles} the noise (mean $\Delta\mathrm{CoV}\approx+0.9$, an increase in
every matched run). On the cold-background phantom, where there is little patient
scatter to re-estimate, the update is essentially inert in both recovery and
noise. Pooled across all datasets the recovery benefit is not statistically
reliable (recovery increased in only $\sim\!53\%$ of matched runs); the update
helps precisely where there is substantial scatter to model, and destabilises the
reconstruction as it does so. The convergence trajectories in
Figure~\ref{fig:brems_scatter_dynamics} make the mechanism explicit: on the warm
phantom the updated-scatter recovery oscillates violently in the early outer
iterations while the noise climbs monotonically, whereas the fixed-scatter
reconstruction is smooth and stable.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/Chapter3/results_scatter_update_dynamics.pdf}
    \caption{Convergence dynamics of the on-the-fly scatter update
    ($\beta=10^{-2}$, range blur on). Recovery coefficient (top) and noise
    (bottom) versus outer iteration for a fixed scatter term (blue) and an
    on-the-fly updated term (orange), on the cold-background (left) and
    warm-background (right) NEMA phantoms. On the warm phantom the updated term
    drives an early recovery overshoot and a monotonic increase in noise; on the
    cold phantom, where there is little scatter to re-estimate, the two strategies
    are almost indistinguishable.}
    \label{fig:brems_scatter_dynamics}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Damping the online update}
\label{subsec:res_damping}

The instability introduced by the online update motivates the damped update rule
of Section~\ref{subsec:rc_update}. Replacing the full update ($\alpha=1$) with a
damped one ($\alpha=0.2$) reduces noise consistently --- in every matched run on
the warm-background and anthropomorphic phantoms (mean
$\Delta\mathrm{CoV}\approx-0.09$ overall). Crucially, in the most unstable
configuration --- the combined updated-scatter\,$+$\,residual strategy on the warm
phantom --- damping improves \emph{both} recovery and noise simultaneously (mean
$\Delta\mathrm{RC}\approx+0.15$, $\Delta\mathrm{CoV}\approx-0.28$), because it
suppresses the iteration-to-iteration oscillation of the undamped update rather
than merely trading recovery for smoothness (Figure~\ref{fig:brems_damping}). In
the already-stable configurations damping behaves as expected, exchanging a small
amount of recovery for reduced noise. The role of damping is therefore best
understood not as a generic smoother but as the stabiliser that renders the
aggressive online update usable.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/Chapter3/results_damping_stability.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of damping the online update on the warm-background NEMA
    phantom, for the (most unstable) updated-scatter\,$+$\,residual strategy at
    $\beta=0$. The full update ($\alpha=1$, red dashed) settles to lower recovery
    and higher noise than the damped update ($\alpha=0.2$, violet solid); damping
    improves both figures of merit by suppressing the inter-iteration
    oscillation.}
    \label{fig:brems_damping}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Bremsstrahlung range blur}
\label{subsec:res_blur}

Including the $6.9\,\mathrm{mm}$ Gaussian range-blur term in the forward model
behaves as a near-``free'' regulariser rather than a resolution-recovery tool.
Across all datasets its dominant, reliable effect is a reduction in noise (noise
fell in every matched run on the warm-background and anthropomorphic phantoms,
mean $\Delta\mathrm{CoV}\approx-0.07$ to $-0.15$), while the accompanying change in
recovery is small and only weakly consistent (mean $\Delta\mathrm{RC}\approx+0.01$,
positive in $\sim\!61\%$ of runs). Figure~\ref{fig:brems_blur_effect} shows this
as off\,$\rightarrow$\,on displacement arrows in the recovery--noise plane: on the
warm phantoms the arrows point predominantly leftward (lower noise) at little cost
in recovery, whereas on the cold phantom --- where counts are low and scatter is
minimal --- the blur has almost no effect. Modelling the range blur is thus
worthwhile because it reduces noise essentially for free, not because it restores
contrast.

    plane. Each arrow runs from ``blur off'' to ``blur on'' for one correction
    strategy at one value of $\beta$; colour denotes strategy. Leftward arrows
    indicate a noise reduction. The effect is consistent and noise-reducing on the

\subsection{Residual correction and resolution recovery}
\label{subsec:res_residual}

Of the four choices, the Fu \& Qi-style residual correction is the most
dependable route to improved recovery. Added to a stable (fixed-scatter) base it
raises recovery in $86\%$ of matched runs (mean $\Delta\mathrm{RC}\approx+0.035$,
with a small spread), at a modest and universal noise cost
(mean $\Delta\mathrm{CoV}\approx+0.09$) --- a textbook resolution-recovery /
noise trade-off. The recovery gain is largest on the warm-background phantom
($\approx+0.10$) and smallest on the cold-background phantom ($\approx+0.02$),
mirroring the scatter-update behaviour. Figure~\ref{fig:brems_residual_size}
resolves the effect by lesion size: the residual correction and the online update
both lift the recovery-versus-diameter curve above the fixed-scatter baseline,
with the largest gains on the mid-to-large spheres. When the residual correction
is instead stacked on the \emph{unstable} online update its average benefit
persists but becomes erratic (its run-to-run spread grows roughly eight-fold),
consistent with the interpretation in Section~\ref{subsec:rc_unmatched} that the
correction is well behaved only when the underlying reconstruction is stable.

\subsection{Overall trade-off and method comparison}
\label{subsec:res_tradeoff}

Sweeping $\beta$ turns each correction strategy into an operating curve in the
recovery--noise plane (Figure~\ref{fig:brems_master_tradeoff}). Read this way, the
strategies separate cleanly on the warm-background and anthropomorphic phantoms
but collapse onto one another on the cold-background phantom, where recovery
saturates near $0.4$ irrespective of the correction applied. On the warm phantoms
the ordering is consistent: the updated-scatter\,$+$\,residual strategy reaches the
highest recovery, followed by the residual-only and updated-only strategies, with
fixed scatter lowest --- but the higher-recovery strategies also sit at higher
noise, so no strategy dominates across the whole curve. The paired-effect summary
of Figure~\ref{fig:brems_effect_summary} condenses the four mechanisms into a
single view: residual correction gives a reliable recovery gain at a fixed noise
cost; the online scatter update offers a large but dataset-dependent recovery gain
at a large noise cost; damping and the range blur are noise-reducers; and every
effect is modulated by whether the background is cold or warm.

\subsection{Clinical example}
\label{subsec:res_patient}

The single patient study is consistent with the phantom findings and, in
particular, behaves like the \emph{warm}-background phantom, as expected for a
liver with diffuse uptake surrounding the lesions
(Figure~\ref{fig:brems_patient}). The online scatter update markedly increases
lesion-to-background contrast relative to a fixed scatter term, again at the price
of higher background noise, reproducing on measured clinical data the trade-off
seen in the warm-background phantom. This should be read qualitatively --- it is a
single subject with no ground-truth activity ratio --- but it demonstrates that
the phantom-derived behaviour transfers to the clinical setting. The effect is
apparent in the reconstructions themselves
(Figure~\ref{fig:brems_recon_patient}): the online update suppresses the diffuse
liver background and lifts the delineated lesion out of it, at the price of a
grainier background, exactly as in the warm-background phantom.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:brems_summary}

Taken together, the four modelling choices form a hierarchy of aggressiveness and
risk. The range blur is a low-risk, noise-reducing addition that is worth
including by default. Residual correction is the dependable way to improve
contrast recovery, provided the underlying reconstruction is stable. The on-the-fly
scatter update offers the largest recovery gains, but only where the background is
warm enough to carry substantial scatter, and it destabilises the reconstruction;
damping is what makes it usable, and in the most unstable configuration recovers
both accuracy and noise simultaneously. No single strategy is universally optimal:
the appropriate choice depends on the imaging task and on where along the
recovery--noise trade-off one wishes to operate. For quantitative liver dosimetry,
where warm backgrounds dominate, a damped online update combined with residual
correction and the range blur, operated at a moderate RDP weight
($\beta\approx10^{-2}$), offers the most favourable balance observed here.
