\chapter{Optimisation for Synergistic Reconstruction}
\label{chapter:optimisation}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} defined the smoothed weighted directional total nuclear variation prior. This chapter examines how to use that prior in joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction with reliable updates and practical computational cost. Two questions organise the chapter: how data subsets and the prior enter stochastic updates, and how data and prior curvature should scale those updates. The general formulation and design alternatives are introduced first, followed by controlled single-bed phantom experiments comparing their behaviour and cost. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} extends this framework to multi-bed patient acquisitions and evaluates reconstruction quality on phantom and clinical data.

\section{Reconstruction Framework}
\label{sec:optimisation_framework}

As in Section~\ref{subsec:synergistic_recon}, let $\bm z=(z_1,z_2)$ again denote the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} images represented on a common reconstruction grid. Chapter~\ref{chapter:reconstruction} presented a native-grid formulation, with the prior applied to $W_m x_m$. Here we optimise the shared-grid variables $z_m$ directly, using the forward model $A_m W_m^\ast z_m+b_m$ detailed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}. The adjoint $W_m^\ast$ maps to the native projector grid; it is not assumed to be an inverse of $W_m$. For $N_{\mathrm d}$ subset data functions, define $\mathcal D(\bm z)=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm d}}\mathcal D_s(\bm z)$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(\bm z)
    =
    \mathcal D(\bm z)
    +
    \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}(\bm z)
    +
    \iota_{\mathcal C}(\bm z),
    \label{eq:optimisation_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal C=(\mathbb R_+^N)^2$ enforces $\bm z\geq0$, so all reconstructed voxel values are non-negative. Here $\mathcal{D}_s$ is the Poisson negative log-likelihood for a data subset, including the relevant projector and image-space warping operator, and $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is the prior from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}.

\section{Stochastic \gls{map} Optimisation}
\label{sec:optimisation_svrg}

Section \ref{sec:optimisation} introduced stochastic optimisation as a promising method for fast and convergent image reconstruction. The stochastic experiments in this chapter use \gls{svrg} \cite{johnsonAcceleratingStochasticGradient2013,twyman_investigation_2023,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. We briefly repeat the main concepts here for convenience. Writing $\widetilde{\mathcal R}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$, define the differentiable part $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal D+\widetilde{\mathcal R}$, so $\mathcal O=\mathcal F+\iota_{\mathcal C}$. Let $N_{\mathrm f}$ denote the total number of sampled functions, including any prior-only components. A component list $\{F_t\}_{t=1}^{N_{\mathrm f}}$ satisfying $\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\mathrm f}}F_t=\mathcal{F}$ is sampled uniformly with replacement, giving an \gls{svrg} estimator at snapshot $\widetilde{\bm z}$,
\[
    g^{(k)}
    =
    \nabla\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    +N_{\mathrm f}\!\left[
        \nabla F_{t_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla F_{t_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad t_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm f}\},
\]
%\SA{Compare this to eq\ref{eq:SVRG1} and remind the reader that $\widetilde{\bm z}$ is a "snapshot" (is that the same as "anchor point" ? ) Will do. Yes- the same as anchor point. Do I use both!? Whoops}
so $\mathbb E[g^{(k)}]=\nabla\mathcal{F}(\bm z^{(k)})$. The full-gradient snapshot is refreshed once per epoch.

The image update is a projected, preconditioned gradient step,
\begin{equation}
    \bm z^{(k+1)}
    =
    \left\{
    \bm z^{(k)}
    -
    \varsigma^{(k)} \mathcal{P}^{(k)} g^{(k)}
    \right\}_{\geq 0},
    \label{eq:optimisation_projected_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}^{(k)}$ is either a diagonal preconditioner or a voxel-wise $2\times2$ block preconditioner coupling the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} update components. The step size is iteration dependent and follows the decaying relaxation rule
\begin{equation}
    \varsigma^{(k)}
    =
    \frac{\varsigma^{(0)}}{1+\eta_{\mathrm{relax}} k},
    \label{eq:optimisation_step_size}
\end{equation}
Here $\varsigma^{(0)}$ is the initial step size and $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}$ controls its decay; their experimental values are specified in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}.

Subset methods accelerate early iterations in emission tomography by replacing full data passes with stochastic or ordered blocks of projection data \cite{hudsonAcceleratedImageReconstruction1994}. Joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction introduces two further choices: corresponding modality subsets can be paired or treated as separate stochastic functions, and the prior can be scheduled relative to those functions in different ways. The alternatives below comprise two subset organisations and three prior-application schedules motivated by prior subset-selection analysis \cite{porterOptimisingSubsetSelection2024a}.

\subsection{Data-Subset Organisation}
\label{subsec:subset_data_organisation}

Let $N_{\mathrm{PET}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ denote the numbers of data subsets for the two modalities. Two organisations are considered.

\paragraph{Separate subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets are treated as distinct stochastic functions. This gives $N_{\mathrm d}=N_{\mathrm{PET}}+N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \{\mathcal{D}_s\}_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm d}}
    =
    \{
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},N_{\mathrm{PET}}},
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}}
    \}.
    \label{eq:separate_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Paired subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets with matching indices are combined into one subset. When the modality counts are equal, this gives $N_{\mathrm d}=N_{\mathrm{PET}}=N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{pair}}_s
    =
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},s}
    +
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},s},
    \qquad s=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm d}.
    \label{eq:paired_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prior-Application Schedules}
\label{subsec:subset_prior_schedules}

For either data organisation, the prior can enter the component list in many ways, of which three were investigated. Each list sums to $\mathcal{F}$ and therefore preserves the unbiasedness of the estimator above.

\paragraph{Folded.}
Here $N_{\mathrm f}=N_{\mathrm d}$, and data subset $s$ corresponds to component $t=s$, with $F_t=\mathcal D_s+\widetilde{\mathcal R}/N_{\mathrm d}$. One expected data pass therefore comprises $N_{\mathrm d}$ updates, with the prior gradient difference entering every update. This gives
\begin{equation}
    g^{(k)} = \nabla\widetilde{\mathcal R}(\bm z^{(k)})
    + \nabla\mathcal D(\widetilde{\bm z})
    + N_{\mathrm d}\!\left[
        \nabla\mathcal D_{s_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla\mathcal D_{s_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad s_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm d}\}.
    \label{eq:svrg_estimator_general}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Prior/half.}
Here $N_{\mathrm f}=2N_{\mathrm d}$: the list contains the $N_{\mathrm d}$ data components and $N_{\mathrm d}$ copies of $\widetilde{\mathcal R}/N_{\mathrm d}$. Uniform sampling selects a prior-only component with probability $1/2$, so an expected data pass comprises $2N_{\mathrm d}$ updates: $N_{\mathrm d}$ data draws and $N_{\mathrm d}$ prior-only draws in expectation.

\paragraph{Once per epoch, on average.}
Here $N_{\mathrm f}=N_{\mathrm d}+1$: one full-prior component $\widetilde{\mathcal R}$ is appended to the $N_{\mathrm d}$ data components. Uniform sampling gives $N_{\mathrm d}+1$ updates per expected data pass, comprising $N_{\mathrm d}$ data draws and one prior-only draw in expectation. The actual number varies because sampling is with replacement. In both intermittent schedules, the snapshot still contains the prior gradient; a prior-only draw updates its correction to the current image.

\section{Prior-Aware Preconditioning}
\label{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}
\label{subsec:preconditioning}

Preconditioning can materially affect \gls{map} reconstruction because the data term and the edge-preserving prior can have very different local curvature scales \cite{clinthornePreconditioningMethodsImproved1993,ahn_globally_2003,depierroFastEMlikeMethods2001,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. The comparison below includes data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling and two prior-aware curvature models for $\wdtnv$, which differ in whether they retain the \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} coupling within each voxel.

\subsection{Data Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_data_preconditioning}

For modality $m$, let $s_m$ denote the sensitivity image on the shared grid,
\begin{equation}
    s_m
    =
    W_m A_m^\top \bm 1,
    \label{eq:shared_grid_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $A_m$ is the native-grid system matrix and the warping operator, $W_m$, maps native images to the shared prior grid. The data-only \gls{em}/\gls{bsrem} preconditioner is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D,m}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left((z_m+\epsilon_x)\oslash s_m\right),
    \label{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
where $\oslash$ denotes element-wise division and $\operatorname{diag}$ forms the diagonal matrix from the resulting voxel-wise scaling values. Here, $\epsilon_x>0$ is a small image stabiliser heuristically chosen to maintain stability whilst allowing zero-valued voxels to be updated. Its subscript identifies image stabilisation, on either a native or a shared grid, and distinguishes it from the prior-smoothing parameter $\epsilon$. Equivalently, the corresponding diagonal data Hessian surrogate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal D,m}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left(s_m \oslash (z_m+\epsilon_x)\right).
    \label{eq:data_hessian_surrogate}
\end{equation}
The labels $\mathcal P_D$ in the figure legends are shorthand for $\mathcal P_{\mathcal D}$. The implementation used in this chapter optionally smooths the numerator image used in the \gls{em} preconditioner with a Gaussian filter, as suggested in \cite{ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}.

\subsection{Combining Data and Prior Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}

Let $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}=\widehat H_{\mathcal D}^{-1}$ denote the data preconditioner and $\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}=\widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{-1}$ a prior preconditioner whose curvature model is developed below. For block methods, the data-curvature block at voxel $n$ is the $2\times2$ diagonal matrix with entries $[\widehat H_{\mathcal D,1}]_{nn}$ and $[\widehat H_{\mathcal D,2}]_{nn}$; its inverse is the corresponding data-preconditioner block. Following \cite{ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}, these curvatures are combined through an inverse-sum, or parallel-sum,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\parallel}
    =
    \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
    =
    \left(\widehat H_{\mathcal D}+\widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}\right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:parallel_sum_combination}
\end{equation}
Numerical floors are used before inversion.

\subsection{Prior Curvature Models}
\label{subsec:optimisation_prior_preconditioning}

Both prior-curvature models start from the exact Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty. Equation~\eqref{eq:phi_gram_spectral} expresses this penalty as a function of the eigenvalues of the symmetric Gram matrix $Y^\top Y$, where $Y$ is the local weighted directional Jacobian. The Lewis--Sendov formula \cite{doi:10.1137/S089547980036838X}, followed by the chain rule through $Y\mapsto Y^\top Y$, therefore gives its local Hessian. This Hessian measures how the penalty gradient changes under perturbations of the local PET and SPECT gradients; its evaluation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:ls_block_assembly}. Both discard curvature between different voxels; they differ in whether the \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} curvature within each voxel is retained. An alternative family, built from a quadratic majoriser of the prior rather than its Hessian, is described in Appendix~\ref{app:mm_preconditioner}.

\subsubsection{Voxel-block curvature}
\label{subsubsec:block_curvature_model}

At each voxel, a $2\times2$ matrix describes how the prior affects the PET and SPECT updates. Its diagonal entries describe each modality separately, while its off-diagonal entries describe their coupling. We approximate these matrices using local second derivatives of the prior and omit coupling between different voxels. Writing $H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}=\nabla^2\widetilde{\mathcal R}$ for the full image-space Hessian, this gives
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}block}}
    =
    \operatorname{blkdiag}
    \left(
        \widehat H_{11}^{\mathrm{LS}},\ldots,\widehat H_{NN}^{\mathrm{LS}}
    \right),
    \qquad
    \widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\approx[H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}]_{nn}\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}.
    \label{eq:ls_block_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
The local derivatives are exact, but their assembly into voxel blocks is approximate, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:ls_block_assembly}. The blocks are positive semidefinite; small eigenvalues are raised to a positive floor before inversion. This avoids singular inverses, but does not by itself guarantee a decrease in the objective. Computing the local derivatives requires voxel-wise singular-value decompositions, so the prior-aware preconditioner is refreshed once per epoch and held fixed between refreshes.

\subsubsection{Diagonal curvature}
\label{subsubsec:diag_curvature_model}

The diagonal model keeps only the diagonal entries of each voxel block,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R},n}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}diag}}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left(
        \bigl[\widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\bigr]_{11},\,
        \bigl[\widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\bigr]_{22}
    \right),
    \label{eq:ls_diag_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
and so discards the cross-modal curvature. These entries are non-negative because each block is positive semidefinite. Combined with the diagonal data curvature, the preconditioner~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} is then diagonal, so component-wise clipping is the compatible projection (Section~\ref{subsubsec:precond_constraint}).

\subsubsection{Interaction with the non-negativity constraint}
\label{subsubsec:precond_constraint}

After each update, negative voxel values are set to zero. With coupled PET/SPECT blocks, this clipping can spoil a direction that would otherwise reduce the objective, so the usual projected-gradient guarantees do not apply. We did not identify a failure attributable to clipping in these experiments, although its effect was not tested separately.

\subsection{Alternative Combination: Lehmer Family}
\label{subsec:optimisation_lehmer}

The inverse sum is dominated by the larger of the two curvatures, so a very large data or prior curvature can leave almost no room to move. The inverse sum belongs to a broader matrix Lehmer family that can relax this. For voxel-wise symmetric positive-definite preconditioners $\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}$, define
\begin{equation}
    T=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1/2}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1/2},
    \qquad
    L_p(\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D})
    =\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{1/2}f_p(T)\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{1/2},
    \qquad
    f_p(t)=\frac{t^p+1}{t^{p-1}+1},
    \label{eq:matrix_lehmer_combination}
\end{equation}
Here $T$ expresses the prior preconditioner relative to the data preconditioner, and $f_p(T)$ is calculated by applying $f_p$ to its eigenvalues.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:optimisation_experiments}

The experiments compare the alternatives defined above while keeping the reconstruction objective fixed. Subset scheduling is assessed with one curvature model; preconditioning is then assessed with one subset schedule. These are controlled comparisons, not a joint search over all settings.

\subsection{Common Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}

The optimisation experiments used an anthropomorphic phantom scanned on the Mediso AnyScan at the National Physical Laboratory. \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} were each partitioned into 18 subsets. Each candidate configuration was evaluated over 20 expected data passes with five stochastic realisations and initial step $\varsigma^{(0)}=1$. The 20-epoch folded, modality-separated runs used $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$; the subset experiment rescales this rate to match relaxation across schedules. A single 1000-epoch reconstruction of the same objective, using a diagonal preconditioner and slower relaxation decay ($\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=10^{-4}$), provided the reference image pair $\bm z^\star$. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_convergence} reports the objective and iterate diagnostics supporting its effective stationarity relative to the best observed iterate.

For each stochastic run and each saved iteration, the \acrfull{nrmse} is evaluated separately for the shared-grid modality images $z_m^{(k)}$ and references $z_m^\star$, with $m=1$ for \gls{pet} and $m=2$ for \gls{spect}. In a shared-grid region $R$, it is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{NRMSE}(z_m^{(k)},z_m^\star;R)
    =
    \frac{\sqrt{|R|^{-1}\sum_{n\in R}(z^{(k)}_{m,n}-z^\star_{m,n})^2}}
         {\sqrt{|R|^{-1}\sum_{n\in R}(z^\star_{m,n})^2}}.
    \label{eq:optimisation_nrmse}
\end{equation}
The regions are evaluated separately over the whole image, the hot-sphere mask, the cold-sphere mask, and the liver compartment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_voi_overlay}. The whole-image region includes the low-activity background surrounding the phantom, and is therefore the most sensitive of the four to behaviour in voxels that carry no clinical information; the liver is the largest structure of interest and the region over which absorbed dose to normal tissue would be scored. The plotted centre line is the arithmetic mean of the five per-run \gls{nrmse}; the envelope is their pointwise minimum--maximum.

\label{sec:optimisation_subset_selection}

The first experiment asks how subset organisation and prior scheduling affect progress towards the reference. It crosses the two data organisations with the three prior schedules, holding the curvature model fixed.

\subsubsection{Results and interpretation}

Figure~\ref{fig:optim_subset_organisation} separates the six configurations mainly by prior schedule. The folded runs reduce mean \gls{nrmse} over the tested 20-pass interval; separate-folded attains the lowest mean \gls{nrmse} at pass 20 in every region for both modalities, with paired-folded close behind. The separate-folded \gls{pet} whole-image \gls{nrmse} curve has a transient jump near pass six before recovering. Neither the sampled nor once-per-epoch schedule shows a net \gls{nrmse} reduction over the same interval.

All three schedules give the same prior correction on average. Folded scheduling applies it at every update, whereas the other schedules apply larger corrections less frequently. These intermittent corrections may contribute to the poorer behaviour observed here, although the experiment also changes how data components are sampled.

The step size was matched at each expected data pass, so different relaxation rates do not explain the result. Update counts and computational work still differ between schedules; this comparison therefore establishes no execution-time advantage.

\subsection{Block versus Diagonal Preconditioning}
\label{subsec:optimisation_block_vs_diag}

The next experiment asks whether incorporating prior curvature improves the updates, and whether retaining within-voxel coupling improves them further. It holds the subset organisation and prior schedule fixed while changing the preconditioner.

\subsubsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{subsec:preconditioner_experiment_methods}

The comparison uses modality-separated subsets and the folded prior schedule, which gave the lowest final mean \gls{nrmse} in the subset experiment. All methods use the common protocol above, with the same objective, initialisation and long-run reference. The data component is smoothed with a $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ Gaussian \gls{fwhm}. Three preconditioners are compared: data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling~\eqref{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}, and its inverse-sum combinations~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} with the block~\eqref{eq:ls_block_preconditioner} and diagonal~\eqref{eq:ls_diag_preconditioner} prior models.

The prior-aware preconditioners are updated once per epoch and held fixed between updates. They therefore use curvature evaluated at the start of the epoch.

All methods minimise the same objective, so the long-run reconstruction provides a common reference $\bm z^\star$. Agreement with this reference is assessed experimentally; it is not a convergence guarantee.

For count-limited \gls{pet} (Figure~\ref{fig:optim_preconditioners}), the whole-image data-only \gls{bsrem} error rises by more than eight orders of magnitude within the first few passes and does not recover over the tested interval. This instability is concentrated in the low-activity background: errors within the hot sphere, cold sphere and liver remain finite, although above the prior-aware trajectories, which decrease in all three regions. The block and diagonal models stay close throughout. The block model gives slightly lower \gls{nrmse} in the liver and, over the first few passes, in the hot sphere; both show transient whole-image increases, near pass 7 for the diagonal model and near pass 14 for the block model.

For \gls{spect}, all three trajectories remain bounded, the block and diagonal models are almost indistinguishable, and data-only \gls{bsrem} gives slightly lower \gls{nrmse} in the anatomical regions. Section~\ref{sec:optimisation_discussion} discusses this contrast with \gls{pet}. The block model's modest \gls{pet} gains led to its use in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}.

Figure~\ref{fig:optim_lehmer_parallel} changes only the combination rule of the block model. The order $p=0.1$ is clearly worse than the inverse sum in every region of both modalities. The order $p=0.01$ is close to the inverse sum. For \gls{pet} it is as good or marginally better in every region, including the hot sphere, and avoids the transient whole-image increase near pass 14, although it shows a smaller one near pass 8. For \gls{spect} it is slightly worse in the hot and cold spheres and slightly better in the liver.

Changing $p$ also changes the effective step scale. The factor $\tfrac12$ is calibrated at $p=0$; for eigenvalues of $T$ of order $10^2$, $p=0.01$ increases the spectral factor by roughly two per cent relative to the inverse sum, rising to a few per cent at $10^3$. A fixed-$\varsigma^{(0)}$ sweep therefore mixes the effect of the Lehmer order with a modest step-size change. Since $p=0.01$ was as good as or marginally better than the inverse sum for \gls{pet}, including the hot sphere, it was retained for Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:optimisation_discussion}

The block and diagonal models behaved similarly. Two mechanisms plausibly limit the benefit of retaining the within-voxel coupling. First, cross-modal curvature is appreciable only where both modalities carry non-negligible local gradients, which may be a small fraction of a volumetric \gls{voi}. Second, wherever the data curvature dominates the inverse sum~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination}, differences between prior models are attenuated. Neither effect was measured.

The modality dependence in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_preconditioners} plausibly reflects the balance between data and prior curvature. The modality weights $\rho_{m,n}$ were chosen in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} to balance the gradients entering the nuclear norm, independently of the modality sensitivities, so the prior/data curvature ratio can differ between \gls{pet} and \gls{spect}. A separate modality scaling of the prior curvature in the preconditioner would change the optimisation metric without changing the objective; it was not investigated here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:optimisation_conclusion}

In the single-bed phantom subset experiment, all schedules used the same smooth objective and an unbiased uniform-sampling \gls{svrg} estimator. We rescaled the relaxation rate from the folded, modality-separated setting $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$, giving every configuration the same step size at a given expected data pass. On that expected data-exposure axis, the folded schedules reduced \gls{nrmse} over 20 expected data passes and the intermittent schedules did not. Since the total update counts differ, this comparison does not establish a wall-clock or compute-efficiency advantage.

Prior-aware preconditioning kept count-limited \gls{pet} bounded over the same 20-pass interval, where data-only \gls{bsrem} diverged; for \gls{spect}, data-only scaling remained competitive. Retaining the within-voxel \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} curvature gave modest \gls{pet} gains over the diagonal model. The Lehmer order $p=0.01$ gave similarly modest \gls{pet} gains over the inverse sum, whereas $p=0.1$ was clearly worse. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} uses the block model with $p=0.01$.
