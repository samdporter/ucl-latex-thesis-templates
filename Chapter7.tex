\chapter{Preconditioning and Subset Selection for Synergistic Reconstruction}
\label{chapter:optimisation}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} defined the smoothed weighted directional total nuclear variation prior. Applying this coupled, edge-preserving prior at scale raises three linked optimisation questions. First, overlapping \gls{pet} bed positions must be reconstructed into a common image if the prior is to act consistently across their overlap. Second, \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets can be paired or treated as separate stochastic functions, and the prior can be folded into every sampled function, sampled alongside the data, or applied once per data pass. Third, the very different data and prior curvature scales require an effective preconditioner. This chapter addresses these questions in turn and uses the resulting evidence to motivate the optimisation protocol carried forward into Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}. %That application protocol had already been frozen before the wider preconditioner comparison reported here.

\section{Multi-Bed \gls{pet} Acquisition}
\label{sec:multibed}

Whole-liver \gls{pet} commonly requires multiple partially overlapping bed positions. Reconstructing each bed independently and fusing the resulting images is awkward for an edge-preserving prior because the regularisation decisions on either side of the overlap are then made independently. In addition, the edge of the field of view of each bed position (where sensitivity is lowest) is often within structures of interest. A joint formulation instead estimates one activity image over the combined field of view.

\subsection{Notation}
\label{subsec:multibed_notation}

Let $x$ be the \gls{pet} activity image on the combined reconstruction grid. For bed position $\mathrm{bed}=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{bed}}$, let $R_{\mathrm{bed}}$ restrict the combined image to the axial support reconstructed by that bed. The expected \gls{pet} data for bed position $\mathrm{bed}$ are
\begin{equation}
    \bar y_{\mathrm{bed}}(x)
    =
    A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}},
    \label{eq:multibed_forward_model}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{bed}}$ is the bed-specific \gls{pet} system matrix and $b_{\mathrm{bed}}$ collects additive corrections. The joint \gls{pet} data term is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}(x)
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \left(
        y_{\mathrm{bed}} \mid A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multibed_data_term}
\end{equation}
with gradient
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}(x)
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    R_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top
    A_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top
    \nabla_{\bar y_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \left(
        y_{\mathrm{bed}} \mid A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \right).
    \label{eq:multibed_gradient}
\end{equation}
The corresponding \gls{pet} sensitivity image on the combined grid is
\begin{equation}
    s_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    R_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top A_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top \bm 1.
    \label{eq:multibed_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
This sensitivity is used in the \gls{em}/\gls{bsrem} data-curvature term in exactly the same way as the single-bed sensitivity in Equation~\eqref{eq:data_hessian_surrogate}.

\subsection{Why Not Stitch Independent Reconstructions?}
\label{subsec:stitching_failure}

A common practical alternative is to reconstruct each \gls{pet} bed independently and fuse the images using sensitivity-weighted stitching in the overlap. With an edge-preserving prior, however, each reconstruction makes its own noise-suppression and edge decisions before fusion. The qualitative example in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_multibed_qualitative} shows overlap-region noise and discontinuities after separate reconstruction and fusion. It is included to motivate the joint formulation; no quantitative comparison was performed \cite{porterSimultaneousMultiBedMAP2025}.

\subsection{Joint Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:joint_multibed}

The joint formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:multibed_data_term} estimates a single combined \gls{pet} image. Each bed contributes its own data term and sensitivity, while the image-domain prior is evaluated over the complete combined field of view. The combined reconstruction therefore permits the edge-preserving prior to act consistently across the overlap, which is the behaviour required for the dual-bed application in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}.

\section{Stochastic \gls{map} Optimisation}
\label{sec:optimisation_svrg}

Let $\bm z=(z_1,z_2)$ denote the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} images represented on a common reconstruction grid. For $N_{\mathrm d}$ sampled data functions, define $\mathcal D(\bm z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm d}}\mathcal D_i(\bm z)$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(\bm z)
    =
    \mathcal D(\bm z)
    +
    \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}(\bm z)
    +
    \iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}(\bm z),
    \label{eq:optimisation_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_i$ is a Poisson negative log-likelihood term composed with the relevant projector, image-space registration operator, and optional resolution model; $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is the prior from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}; and $\iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}$ is the indicator function of the non-negative orthant,
\begin{equation}
    \iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}(\bm z)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        0, & \bm z \geq 0,\\
        +\infty, & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:nonneg_indicator}
\end{equation}
with the inequality understood componentwise. As $\iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}$ is non-smooth, it is kept out of the gradient estimator $g^{(k)}$ below and instead handled by proximal (projected) gradient splitting: the smooth terms $\mathcal D+\mathcal R$ are advanced by a stochastic gradient step, and the result is passed through $\operatorname{prox}_{\varsigma^{(k)}\iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}}$, which for an indicator function reduces to Euclidean projection onto $\mathbb{R}_+$, i.e.\ clipping negative voxels to zero. This is exactly the $\{\cdot\}_{\geq 0}$ operation in the update Equation~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update}.

The stochastic experiments use \gls{svrg} \cite{johnsonAcceleratingStochasticGradient2013,twymanInvestigationStochasticVariance2022,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025}. Writing $\mathcal R=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$, the implemented estimator keeps the complete prior outside the sampler. At anchor point $\widetilde{\bm z}$ it is
\begin{equation}
    g^{(k)} = \nabla\mathcal R(\bm z^{(k)})
    + \nabla\mathcal D(\widetilde{\bm z})
    + N_{\mathrm d}\!\left[
        \nabla\mathcal D_{i_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla\mathcal D_{i_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad i_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm d}\}.
    \label{eq:svrg_estimator_general}
\end{equation}
The data functions are sampled uniformly with replacement. The complete prior gradient is evaluated at every stochastic update, and the full data-gradient snapshot is refreshed once per epoch. The subset-selection experiment in Section~\ref{sec:optimisation_subset_selection} also evaluates coarser prior schedules that keep the same average prior weight per data pass.

The image update is a projected, preconditioned gradient step,
\begin{equation}
    \bm z^{(k+1)}
    =
    \left\{
    \bm z^{(k)}
    -
    \varsigma^{(k)} \mathcal{P}^{(k)} g^{(k)}
    \right\}_{\geq 0},
    \label{eq:optimisation_projected_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}^{(k)}$ is either a diagonal preconditioner or a voxel-wise $2\times2$ block preconditioner coupling the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} update components. The step size is iteration dependent and follows the linear decay rule
\begin{equation}
    \varsigma^{(k)}
    =
    \frac{\varsigma^{(0)}}{1+\eta_{\mathrm{relax}} k},
    \qquad
    \varsigma^{(0)}=1,
    \qquad
    \eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002,
    \label{eq:optimisation_step_size}
\end{equation}
for the 20-pass comparisons described below.

\subsection{Common Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}

The convergence experiments used an anthropomorphic phantom scanned on the Mediso AnyScan at the National Physical Laboratory. \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} were each partitioned into 18 subsets. Each plotted comparison curve comprises five stochastic realisations and covers the first 20 complete data passes. A single 1000-epoch reconstruction of the same objective, run with more conservative optimisation settings, provided the reference image $x^\star$. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_convergence} reports the objective and iterate diagnostics supporting its effective stationarity relative to the best observed iterate.

For each stochastic run and each saved iteration, the \acrfull{nrmse} in region $R$ is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{NRMSE}(x_k,x^\star;R)
    =
    \frac{\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_{j\in R}(x_{k,j}-x^\star_j)^2}}
         {\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_{j\in R}(x^\star_j)^2}},
    \qquad N=|R|.
    \label{eq:optimisation_nrmse}
\end{equation}
The regions are evaluated separately over the whole image, the hot-sphere mask, the cold-sphere mask, and the liver compartment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_voi_overlay}. The whole-image region includes the low-activity background surrounding the phantom, and is therefore the most sensitive of the four to behaviour in voxels that carry no clinical information; the liver is the largest structure of interest and the region over which absorbed dose to normal tissue would be scored. Reporting both separates a failure confined to the background from one that reaches the anatomy. The plotted centre line is the arithmetic mean of the five per-run \gls{nrmse} values at each iteration, not the \gls{nrmse} of an averaged reconstruction; the envelope is their pointwise minimum--maximum. The long-run reference itself was generated once.

\section{Subset Selection}
\label{sec:optimisation_subset_selection}

Subset methods accelerate early iterations in emission tomography by replacing full data passes with stochastic or ordered blocks of projection data \cite{hudsonAcceleratedImageReconstruction1994}. Joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction introduces two further choices. Corresponding modality subsets can be added to form paired stochastic functions, or each modality subset can remain a separate function; and the prior can be applied on different schedules relative to the sampled data functions. The experiment below crosses these two subset organisations with three prior-application schedules. All three schedules deliver the same average prior weight, one complete prior contribution per data pass, and differ only in how finely that contribution is distributed across updates. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citation for IEEE Xplore document 10654962.}

\subsection{Data-Subset Organisation}
\label{subsec:subset_data_organisation}

The one-bed phantom data were divided into 18 \gls{pet} and 18 \gls{spect} subsets. Two organisations were tested.

\paragraph{Separate subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets are treated as distinct stochastic functions. For a one-bed acquisition with $N_{\mathrm{PET}}=18$ and $N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}=18$, this gives
\begin{equation}
    \{\mathcal{D}_i\}_{i=1}^{36}
    =
    \{
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},18},
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},18}
    \}.
    \label{eq:separate_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Paired subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets with matching indices are summed into one stochastic function. For one bed this gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{pair}}_j
    =
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},j}
    +
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},j},
    \qquad j=1,\ldots,18.
    \label{eq:paired_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prior-Application Schedules}
\label{subsec:subset_prior_schedules}

Writing $N_{\mathrm d}$ for the number of data functions in the chosen organisation ($N_{\mathrm d}=36$ separate, $N_{\mathrm d}=18$ paired), three schedules for applying the prior $\mathcal R$ were compared. All runs use the \gls{svrg} estimator.

\paragraph{Folded.}
The scaled prior $\mathcal R/N_{\mathrm d}$ is added to every data function, so each sampled function is $\mathcal D_i + \mathcal R/N_{\mathrm d}$ and every update evaluates the complete prior gradient. Because the prior enters every sampled function with the same weight, the estimator reduces algebraically to Equation~\eqref{eq:svrg_estimator_general}: folding is mathematically identical to holding the complete prior outside the sampler and evaluating it at every update. This is the schedule used by the preconditioner experiments later in this chapter and by the application protocol of Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}.

\paragraph{Sampled.}
The prior, scaled by $1/N_{\mathrm d}$, is kept outside the data functions and instead enters the sampler as additional stochastic functions on the same footing as the data. Prior-only updates therefore occur on half of the draws in expectation, roughly every other update, and accumulate one complete prior contribution per data pass on average. This schedule is labelled prior/half in the figure legend.

\paragraph{Once per epoch.}
The sampled data updates exclude the prior entirely; the complete prior gradient is instead applied in a single dedicated update once per data pass.

\subsection{Experiment Design and Work Accounting}
\label{subsec:subset_selection_experiment_methods}

All stochastic functions were sampled uniformly with replacement. To compare the configurations on a common horizontal axis, one matched data pass is defined by 36 modality-specific data-subset evaluations. Paired organisation reaches this count after 18 joint \gls{pet}+\gls{spect} data updates, each evaluating one \gls{pet} and one \gls{spect} subset; separate organisation reaches it after 36 modality-specific data updates. Prior work per matched pass then depends on the schedule. The folded schedule evaluates the complete prior gradient at every data update (36 evaluations per pass separate, 18 paired). The sampled schedule interleaves on average as many prior-only updates as data updates, roughly doubling the parameter-update count per pass for a comparable number of prior-gradient evaluations. The once-per-epoch schedule adds a single prior-gradient evaluation and update per pass.

This accounting matches data-projection work and average prior weight, but it does not match parameter-update count, prior-gradient work, or elapsed computation. Prior-only updates do not advance the horizontal axis, and a prior-gradient evaluation is much cheaper than a data-subset evaluation, so the additional prior work of the folded and sampled schedules, up to $N_{\mathrm d}$ evaluations per pass, is not reflected in the plotted axis. All configurations used the same objective and the \gls{mm} local-diagonal curvature preconditioner, and were assessed with the common 20-pass protocol in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}.

\subsection{Results}

Figure~\ref{fig:optim_subset_organisation} separates the six configurations by prior schedule far more than by subset organisation. Only the folded schedule converged. Separate folded subsets reached the lowest \gls{nrmse} in every region for both modalities, with paired folded subsets close behind and the clearest gaps in the three anatomical regions; the separate folded \gls{pet} whole-image mean shows one large transient excursion near pass six before recovering. Under the sampled and once-per-epoch schedules, neither organisation made net progress within the 20 passes: the curves stagnate near their starting values, with the once-per-epoch runs additionally showing large excursions and, for \gls{spect}, early transient progress that subsequently regressed. Because all six configurations deliver the same average prior weight per matched data pass, the failures are attributable to the granularity of prior application rather than to its total contribution.

Two established principles account for this. The first is the subset gradient balance condition underlying all incremental-gradient and ordered-subset methods: a subset step is useful only when the scaled subset gradient approximates the full gradient, $\nabla\mathcal{O}(\bm z)\approx N_{\mathrm d}\nabla\mathcal{O}_i(\bm z)$, and imbalanced partitions degrade or destroy convergence \cite{hudsonAcceleratedImageReconstruction1994}. In tomography this condition is normally discussed for projection-angle subsets, where balance means interleaving widely separated angles rather than grouping contiguous ones. A prior-only sampled function is the extreme violation of the same condition, because its gradient shares no structure with the data gradient by construction; the sampled and once-per-epoch schedules impose exactly that partition. This explains why matching the average prior weight does not rescue them: balance is a property of the individual draws, not of their mean. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citation for Fessler, \emph{Image Reconstruction: Algorithms and Analysis}, Section 14.10, where the subset gradient balance condition and the consequences of imbalanced subsets are set out.}

The second is that under \gls{svrg} all three schedules are unbiased, so the mechanism must be variance rather than bias. In the folded schedule the complete prior gradient is evaluated deterministically at every update and contributes no variance to $g^{(k)}$ at all; in the sampled and once-per-epoch schedules the prior contribution is itself a random variable whose realisations are inflated by the sampler scaling. The observed stagnation is therefore best characterised as excess estimator variance concentrated in a direction that the data term does not oppose, rather than as the prior-only updates disturbing the iterates in some unspecified way. A direct measurement of $\operatorname{Var}[g^{(k)}]$ under the three schedules would confirm this and was not attempted here.

Within the folded schedule, the organisation effect is plausibly an update-frequency effect: the separate organisation performs 36 parameter updates per matched data pass, compared with 18 paired updates, and therefore makes more frequent progress for the same number of data-subset projections. The experiment does not isolate modality grouping from update frequency, and should be interpreted as a data-projection-matched comparison of the implemented configurations, not as a compute-matched speed benchmark or a causal test of modality splitting alone. All runs used the \gls{mm} local-diagonal curvature preconditioner with the common step-size schedule, so the schedule sensitivity reported here is an observation under that protocol rather than an established general property.

\section{Prior-Aware Preconditioning}
\label{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}
\label{subsec:preconditioning}

Preconditioning is critical for subset-based \gls{map} reconstruction because the data term and the edge-preserving prior can have very different local curvature scales \cite{clinthornePreconditioningMethodsImproved1993,ahnGloballyConvergentOrdered2002,depierroFastEMlikeMethods2001,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025}. The comparison below includes data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling and four prior-aware curvature models for $\wdtnv$.

\subsection{Data Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_data_preconditioning}

For modality $m$, let $s_m$ denote the sensitivity image on the shared grid,
\begin{equation}
    s_m
    =
    W_m A_m^\top \bm 1,
    \label{eq:shared_grid_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $A_m$ is the native-grid system matrix and $W_m$ maps native images to the shared prior grid. The data-only \gls{em}/\gls{bsrem} preconditioner is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{BSREM},m}
    =
    (z_m+\epsilon_x)\oslash s_m,
    \label{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
with element-wise division. This scaling models the data term only; it contains no prior curvature. Equivalently, the corresponding diagonal data Hessian surrogate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathrm{data},m}
    =
    s_m \oslash (z_m+\epsilon_x).
    \label{eq:data_hessian_surrogate}
\end{equation}
The implementation optionally smooths the numerator image used in the \gls{em} preconditioner with a Gaussian filter. In the experiments below the smoothing \gls{fwhm} is $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in each direction.

\subsection{Prior Curvature Models}
\label{subsec:optimisation_prior_preconditioning}

Four prior-curvature constructions were considered: three based on a local \acrfull{mm}/\acrfull{irls} surrogate and one based on the Lewis--Sendov Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty.

\subsubsection{A quadratic majoriser in Jacobian space}
\label{subsubsec:jacobian_majoriser}

The \gls{mm} label applied to the first family is not merely descriptive of how the weights were derived. At the level of the voxel-wise Jacobian the majorisation can be established exactly, and doing so identifies what the implemented preconditioners approximate and where the approximation begins.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:jacobian_majoriser}
Let $Y,Y_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}$ with $d\geq M$, let $g$ be the Charbonnier function of Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} and let $\phi(Y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}g(\sigma_\ell(Y))$. Define
\begin{equation}
    C_0 := \Omegaeps{Y_0} = \left(Y_0^\top Y_0 + \epsilon^2\mathbb{I}_M\right)^{-1/2}.
    \label{eq:majoriser_weight}
\end{equation}
Then, for all $Y$,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(Y)
    \;\leq\;
    \phi(Y_0)
    +\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(Y^\top Y - Y_0^\top Y_0\right)C_0\right],
    \label{eq:jacobian_majoriser}
\end{equation}
with equality at $Y=Y_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The eigenvalues of $Y^\top Y$ are $\sigma_\ell(Y)^2$ for $\ell=1,\dots,M$, so
\begin{equation}
    \phi(Y)
    =\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\left(\sqrt{\sigma_\ell(Y)^2+\epsilon^2}-\epsilon\right)
    =\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\left(Y^\top Y+\epsilon^2\mathbb{I}_M\right)^{1/2}\right)-M\epsilon .
    \label{eq:phi_as_trace}
\end{equation}
The map $t\mapsto t^{1/2}$ is operator concave on the positive semidefinite cone \cite{bhatiaMatrixAnalysis1997a}, and the trace is linear and monotone, so $A\mapsto\operatorname{tr}((A+\epsilon^2\mathbb{I})^{1/2})$ is concave in $A\succeq0$. A differentiable concave function lies below its first-order expansion, and its gradient is $\tfrac12(A+\epsilon^2\mathbb{I})^{-1/2}$. Setting $A=Y^\top Y$ and $A_0=Y_0^\top Y_0$ and adding the constant $-M\epsilon$ gives~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser}; equality at $Y=Y_0$ is immediate.
\end{proof}

Three consequences matter here. First, the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser} is a convex quadratic in $Y$, because $Y^\top Y$ is a convex quadratic and $C_0\succ0$; it is therefore a surrogate in the standard sense of lying above $\phi$ and touching it at the current point. Second, its gradient at $Y=Y_0$ is $Y_0C_0$, which is exactly the gradient derived in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} (Equation~\eqref{eq:2x2_gram}), so the surrogate satisfies the matched-tangent condition automatically. Third, and most usefully, the weight $C_0$ is the matrix-valued analogue of the optimal curvature of a scalar edge-preserving potential. For a symmetric potential $\psi$ whose curvature $\omega_\psi(s)=\dot\psi(s)/s$ is non-increasing on $(0,\infty)$, the parabola with curvature $\omega_\psi$ is the smallest-curvature quadratic surrogate \cite{huberRobustEstimationLocation1964a}; for the hyperbola potential $\psi(s)=\sqrt{s^2+\epsilon^2}$ this gives $\omega_\psi(s)=1/\sqrt{s^2+\epsilon^2}$, which is precisely the scalar $M=1$ case of~\eqref{eq:majoriser_weight}. The construction used here is thus the spectral generalisation of the multiplicative form of half-quadratic regularisation, which is itself a rediscovery of Huber's quadratic surrogates. That the same object serves as both gradient weight and curvature is a structural consequence of the majorisation, not a coincidence of implementation. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citations for Fessler, \emph{Image Reconstruction: Algorithms and Analysis}, Theorem 14.4.5 (optimality of Huber's curvature $\omega_\psi$) and Section 14.4.4.5 (half-quadratic methods as Huber surrogates), together with Charbonnier et al.\ (1997) and Nikolova and Ng (2005) on the multiplicative and additive half-quadratic forms.}

Summing~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser} over voxels yields a quadratic majoriser of the complete prior whose Hessian has the form $\bm{D}^\ast(\cdot)\bm{D}$, where $\bm{D}$ collects the weighted directional finite-difference operators $\rho_{m,n}\Pi_{m,n}D$ over all modalities and voxels. It is distinct from the data-fidelity term $\mathcal{D}$ of~\eqref{eq:optimisation_objective}. This majoriser is not separable: it couples each voxel to its finite-difference neighbours. Every preconditioner implemented below reduces it further, and Section~\ref{subsubsec:depierro_gap} identifies exactly what that reduction costs.

For the \gls{mm} family, let $k_0$ denote a preconditioner-refresh iteration and, at voxel $n$, define the anchored weighted directional Jacobian
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n^{(k_0)}
    :=
    \tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n\!\left(\bm z^{(k_0)}\right)
    =
    \left[
        \rho_{1,n}\Pi_{1,n}(Dz_1^{(k_0)})_n
        \;\middle|\;
        \rho_{2,n}\Pi_{2,n}(Dz_2^{(k_0)})_n
    \right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}
    \label{eq:optimisation_weighted_jacobian}
\end{equation}
be the weighted directional Jacobian from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} (Equation~\eqref{eq:Yn_weighted}). The active code forms the modality-space \glsentryshort{mm} weight
\begin{equation}
    C_n^{(k_0)}
    =
    \Omegaeps{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n^{(k_0)}}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        c_{11,n}^{(k_0)} & c_{12,n}^{(k_0)}\\
        c_{12,n}^{(k_0)} & c_{22,n}^{(k_0)}
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:mm_weight_current_code}
\end{equation}
This is the same smoothed spectral weight~\eqref{eq:spectral_weight} that supplies the gradient in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} (Equation~\eqref{eq:2x2_gram}), now evaluated at the refresh anchor and read as the local quadratic-surrogate weight. The three uses differ only in where the map is evaluated: at a generic $Y$ for the gradient, at the frozen anchor $Y_0$ for the majoriser, and at $\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n^{(k_0)}$ per voxel in the implementation. The weight is held fixed while the trial variable changes and until the next preconditioner refresh. To keep the formulas below readable, the refresh superscript is suppressed there, so $C_n$ and $c_{mm',n}$ denote the anchored quantities $C_n^{(k_0)}$ and $c_{mm',n}^{(k_0)}$.

For diagonal preconditioning, the finite-difference stencil and directional projectors are collapsed to a per-voxel participation factor $\tau_{m,n}$. This factor includes the number of valid forward/backward stencil incidences at voxel $n$, the squared finite-difference scale factors, and the diagonal entries of $\Pi_{m,n}^\top\Pi_{m,n}$. The local diagonal \wdtnv{} curvature approximation is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,m,n}^{\mathrm{loc\text{-}diag}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}\,
    \rho_{m,n}^2\,c_{mm,n}\,\tau_{m,n}.
    \label{eq:diag_tight_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
This is the diagonal extraction of the local \glsentryshort{mm} curvature surrogate. A more conservative Gershgorin-inflated diagonal surrogate adds the magnitude of the cross-modal term,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,1,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}G}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}\,
    \rho_{1,n}^2\,(c_{11,n}+|c_{12,n}|)\,\tau_{1,n},
    \qquad
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,2,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}G}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}\,
    \rho_{2,n}^2\,(c_{22,n}+|c_{12,n}|)\,\tau_{2,n}.
    \label{eq:diag_gersh_preconditioner}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Diagonal extraction versus a majorising diagonal}
\label{subsubsec:depierro_gap}

Equations~\eqref{eq:diag_tight_preconditioner} and~\eqref{eq:diag_gersh_preconditioner} extract a diagonal from the non-separable surrogate Hessian implied by Proposition~\ref{prop:jacobian_majoriser}. Diagonal extraction does not preserve majorisation, and the classical route that does is De Pierro's convexity trick \cite{depierroModifiedExpectationMaximization1995a}. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citation for Erdo\u{g}an and Fessler (1999) on ordered-subsets separable paraboloidal surrogates, where De Pierro's trick is applied to penalised-likelihood tomography.} For a quadratic surrogate with Hessian $\mathcal{B}^\top\mathrm{diag}\{\breve c_i\}\mathcal{B}$, writing $[\mathcal{B}v]_i=\sum_j b_{ij}v_j$ as a convex combination with weights $\pi_{ij}=|b_{ij}|/b_i$, where $b_i=\sum_j|b_{ij}|$ is the row $\ell_1$ norm, gives the separable majorising diagonal
\begin{equation}
    d_j^{\mathrm{DP}}
    =
    \sum_i \frac{|b_{ij}|^2}{\pi_{ij}}\,\breve c_i
    =
    \sum_i |b_{ij}|\,b_i\,\breve c_i ,
    \qquad\text{compared with}\qquad
    d_j^{\mathrm{diag}}
    =
    \sum_i b_{ij}^2\,\breve c_i .
    \label{eq:depierro_vs_diag}
\end{equation}
The two differ by the replacement of $|b_{ij}|$ with the row $\ell_1$ norm $b_i$, so $d_j^{\mathrm{DP}}\geq d_j^{\mathrm{diag}}$ always, with the inflation factor equal to a $\breve c$-weighted average of $b_i/|b_{ij}|$. For an isotropic first-order difference, whose rows contain exactly the two entries $\pm1/\Delta_\ell$, this factor is exactly $2$. Once $\Pi_{m,n}$ mixes difference directions the rows acquire up to $2d$ non-zeros and the factor grows accordingly, so in three dimensions the gap lies between $2$ and $2d=6$ depending on the guidance strength $\gamma_m$.

Two consequences follow. First, the Gershgorin construction~\eqref{eq:diag_gersh_preconditioner} inflates along the \emph{modality} index, adding $|c_{12,n}|$ to the diagonal of the $2\times2$ block $C_n$. The off-diagonal mass that actually obstructs diagonal dominance of the surrogate Hessian is \emph{spatial}, arising from the neighbour coupling in $\bm{D}^\ast(\cdot)\bm{D}$. The Gershgorin variant therefore reinforces the wrong axis, which offers a plausible explanation for why it tracks the tight diagonal so closely in Section~\ref{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}.

Second, because the implemented diagonal is smaller than a majorising diagonal, the corresponding preconditioner is correspondingly larger, so the descent guarantee that \gls{mm} would otherwise supply is lost by a bounded and known factor. Substituting the right-hand form of~\eqref{eq:depierro_vs_diag} for the diagonal used in~\eqref{eq:diag_tight_preconditioner} would restore it, at the cost of a more conservative step. This substitution is a change to one diagonal and would be the first construction in this chapter with a majorisation argument running end to end; it was not implemented here.

For block preconditioning, the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} update components at each voxel are coupled by a $2\times2$ block. Let $\mathcal{N}(n)$ be the set of target voxels whose finite-difference stencil depends on source voxel $n$. For target voxel $r\in\mathcal{N}(n)$, let $c_{r\leftarrow n}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the finite-difference impulse vector produced in the directional channels when source voxel $n$ is perturbed. The local voxel-block curvature approximation uses
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{mm'}
    =
    c_{r\leftarrow n}^\top
    \Pi_{m,r}^\top
    \Pi_{m',r}
    c_{r\leftarrow n},
    \label{eq:block_alpha}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,n}^{\mathrm{loc\text{-}block}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{r\in\mathcal{N}(n)}
    \left[
        B_r C_r B_r
    \right]
    \circ
    A_{r\leftarrow n},
    \qquad
    A_{r\leftarrow n}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{11} & \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{12}\\
        \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{21} & \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{22}
    \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:block_tight_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
where $B_r=\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{1,r},\rho_{2,r})$ and $\circ$ denotes the Hadamard product.

The fourth construction uses the exact Lewis--Sendov Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty \cite{lewisDerivativesSpectralFunctions1996}. If $H_{\mathcal R}=\nabla^2\mathcal R$ denotes the resulting image-space Hessian, the implemented approximation retains only its voxel-wise diagonal blocks,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}block}}
    =
    \operatorname{blkdiag}
    \left(
        [H_{\mathcal R}]_{11},\ldots,[H_{\mathcal R}]_{NN}
    \right),
    \qquad
    [H_{\mathcal R}]_{nn}\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}.
    \label{eq:ls_block_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
It therefore retains \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} cross-curvature within each voxel but discards spatial off-diagonal blocks. Evaluating the smoothed spectral Hessian requires the voxel-wise singular-value decompositions used by the Lewis--Sendov construction. Recomputing this block model once per epoch consequently has a non-negligible cost.

This fourth construction differs from the other three in kind and not only in detail. The first three approximate a majoriser, whereas the Lewis--Sendov block model approximates the exact Hessian at the current iterate, and is therefore Newton-like rather than \gls{mm}-like. Because $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is convex, $H_{\mathcal R}\succeq0$ and the resulting preconditioner is well defined, but the local Hessian of a non-quadratic function does not majorise it, so no descent property is available even in principle. The distinction is worth stating because Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} has this construction frozen into its protocol.

\subsubsection{Interaction with the non-negativity constraint}
\label{subsubsec:precond_constraint}

The block constructions~\eqref{eq:block_tight_preconditioner} and~\eqref{eq:ls_block_preconditioner} raise a convergence-theoretic issue that the diagonal constructions do not. The update~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update} is a projected preconditioned gradient step, and it is a classical result that an arbitrary preconditioner cannot be inserted into a projected gradient method: for a general positive-definite $\mathcal{P}$ the iteration need not converge to a constrained minimiser, even for small step sizes, and counterexamples exist in which the ideal unconstrained choice $\mathcal{P}=H^{-1}$ fails completely by leaving the iterates stranded on the boundary. For the non-negative orthant, certain \emph{diagonal} scalings are admissible, which is what underwrites the convergence of \gls{bsrem}-type and separable-surrogate methods. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citations for Bertsekas, \emph{Nonlinear Programming} (2nd ed.), and Fessler, \emph{Image Reconstruction: Algorithms and Analysis}, Section 12.2.2 and Examples 12.2.2 and 12.3.1, which give the counterexamples for preconditioned and Newton-type projected gradient under non-negativity.}

The relevance here is direct. The non-negativity constraint is separable across voxels and modalities, whereas the voxel-block preconditioners couple the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} components within each voxel. The projection in~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update} therefore does not commute with the preconditioned step in the way that the diagonal theory requires, and the standard guarantee does not transfer. Three factors mitigate this in practice: the retained blocks are $2\times2$ rather than a full inverse Hessian, they are combined with the data curvature by the inverse sum of Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}, and the relaxed step size drives $\varsigma^{(k)}\to0$. None of these constitutes a proof. The constraint is also active most often in the count-limited \gls{pet} background, which is precisely where the empirical behaviour reported below is least stable, so this should be regarded as an open question about the block methods rather than a settled point.

\subsection{Combining Data and Prior Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}

Let $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}=\widehat H_{\mathrm D}^{-1}$ denote the data preconditioner and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}=\widehat H_{\mathcal R}^{-1}$ the preconditioner obtained from one of the prior-curvature models above. For block methods, $\widehat H_{\mathrm D}$ is the $2\times2$ diagonal matrix containing the modality-specific data curvatures at each voxel. The principal comparison combines the operands through the inverse-sum, or parallel-sum, rule
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\parallel}
    =
    \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
    =
    \left(\widehat H_{\mathrm D}+\widehat H_{\mathcal R}\right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:parallel_sum_combination}
\end{equation}
The same expression applies to diagonal and voxel-block operands, with numerical floors used before inversion.

The inverse sum is not an arbitrary choice among plausible combination rules. Surrogate curvatures are additive: if $\phi_{\mathrm D}$ majorises the data term with Hessian $\widehat H_{\mathrm D}$ and $\phi_{\mathcal R}$ majorises the prior with Hessian $\widehat H_{\mathcal R}$, then $\phi_{\mathrm D}+\phi_{\mathcal R}$ majorises $\mathcal D+\mathcal R$ with Hessian $\widehat H_{\mathrm D}+\widehat H_{\mathcal R}$, and the corresponding \gls{mm} step preconditions by the inverse of that sum. This is the same assembly used when separable quadratic surrogates are built term by term for penalised-likelihood problems. \TODO{Add the Zenodo-managed citations for Erdo\u{g}an and Fessler (1999) and for Fessler, \emph{Image Reconstruction: Algorithms and Analysis}, Section 14.5.7, on term-by-term assembly of separable quadratic surrogates.} Equation~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} is therefore the combination that the surrogate framework prescribes, and any rule that is not an inverse sum of curvatures forfeits that interpretation. What is \emph{not} claimed is that either operand majorises its own term here, since Section~\ref{subsubsec:depierro_gap} showed the implemented diagonals fall short of a majorising diagonal; the combination inherits the approximation of its operands.

The preconditioner is updated once per epoch and held fixed between updates. This limits curvature-computation cost, particularly for the Lewis--Sendov construction, but means the preconditioner becomes progressively stale within each epoch.

\subsection{Modality Asymmetry in the Prior/Data Curvature Balance}
\label{subsec:modality_curvature_balance}

That the same preconditioner is necessary for \gls{pet} and over-damping for \gls{spect} is not surprising once the two operands of the inverse sum~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} are compared per modality. The influence of the prior on modality $m$'s step is governed by the ratio $\widehat H_{\mathcal R,m}/\widehat H_{\mathrm D,m}$, and nothing in the present construction controls that ratio.

The prior curvature~\eqref{eq:diag_tight_preconditioner} carries a factor $\rho_{m,n}^2$, whereas the data curvature~\eqref{eq:data_hessian_surrogate} does not involve $\rho$ at all:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\widehat H_{\mathcal R,m,n}}{\widehat H_{\mathrm D,m,n}}
    =
    \frac{V_{\mathrm{vox}}\,\rho_{m,n}^{2}\,c_{mm,n}\,\tau_{m,n}\,(z_{m,n}+\epsilon_x)}{s_{m,n}} .
    \label{eq:curvature_ratio}
\end{equation}
The explicit $\rho_{m,n}^2$ is partially offset by $c_{mm,n}$, which is itself $\rho$-dependent because the \gls{mm} weight~\eqref{eq:mm_weight_current_code} is formed from the \emph{weighted} Jacobian. Where directional gradients are large relative to $\epsilon$, $c_{mm,n}$ falls approximately as $1/\rho_{m,n}$ and the net dependence is close to linear; where they are small, $c_{mm,n}\to1/\epsilon$ and the dependence is close to quadratic. The net scaling therefore lies between linear and quadratic in $\rho_{m,n}$ and varies spatially with the local gradient regime.

What matters for the comparison is that the data curvature carries \emph{no} $\rho$ dependence whatsoever. The modality weights were chosen in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} to equalise the gradient magnitudes entering the nuclear norm, a prior-modelling criterion evaluated without reference to either modality's sensitivity image, so nothing in the present construction keeps~\eqref{eq:curvature_ratio} comparable between \gls{pet} and \gls{spect}. The observed asymmetry, prior curvature being necessary for \gls{pet} and mildly counterproductive for \gls{spect}, is consistent with \gls{spect} sitting at the over-damped end of this ratio, though the experiments reported here do not measure~\eqref{eq:curvature_ratio} directly and so do not establish that as the cause.

This suggests a targeted remedy that is more specific than retuning the shared step schedule: decouple the preconditioner weighting from the prior weighting by introducing a modality-specific curvature scaling in the combination,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\parallel,m}
    =
    \left(\widehat H_{\mathrm D,m}+\lambda_m \widehat H_{\mathcal R,m}\right)^{-1},
    \qquad \lambda_m>0,
    \label{eq:modality_precond_weight}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{PET}}=1$ recovering the present scheme and $\lambda_{\mathrm{SPECT}}<1$ relaxing the damping applied to the more robust modality. Because $\lambda_m$ enters only the preconditioner and not the objective, this changes the geometry seen by the optimiser without altering the estimator, and so is distinct from retuning $\rho_{m,n}$, which would change the prior itself. Selecting $\lambda_m$, for instance by matching~\eqref{eq:curvature_ratio} across modalities over the support mask, was not attempted here and is left as future work.

\subsection{Lehmer Combination}

The inverse sum is one member of a broader matrix Lehmer family. For the voxel-wise symmetric positive-definite preconditioners $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}$, define
\begin{equation}
    T=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1/2}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1/2},
    \qquad
    L_p(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D})
    =\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{1/2}f_p(T)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{1/2},
    \qquad
    f_p(t)=\frac{t^p+1}{t^{p-1}+1},
    \label{eq:matrix_lehmer_combination}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the congruence transform of $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}$ by $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1/2}$, unrelated to the \gls{mm} weight $C_n$ of~\eqref{eq:mm_weight_current_code}, and $f_p(T)$ is evaluated spectrally. The implemented preconditioner is $\tfrac12L_p(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D})$. At $p=0$ this reduces exactly to the parallel sum,
\begin{equation}
    \tfrac12L_0(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D})
    =
    \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}^{-1}\right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:lehmer_parallel_equivalence}
\end{equation}

Two caveats limit what should be read into the $p$ sweep. First, only $p=0$ carries the surrogate interpretation of Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}, in which curvatures add; other orders are combination rules without a corresponding majorisation argument. Second, the factor $\tfrac12$ in $\tfrac12L_p$ is calibrated so that $p=0$ reproduces the parallel sum exactly, which means the overall scale of the combination drifts with $p$. Evaluating $\tfrac12 f_p$ against $f_0$ spectrally, the Lehmer combination at $p=0.01$ exceeds the parallel sum by roughly two per cent where the eigenvalues of $T$ are of order $10^2$, rising to a few per cent at $10^3$; that is, $p>0$ acts as a mild step-size inflation wherever prior curvature is small relative to data curvature. A sweep over $p$ at fixed $\varsigma^{(0)}$ therefore confounds the shape of the combination with a small change in effective step size, and the modest advantage observed here is consistent with either. Separating them would require re-tuning $\varsigma^{(0)}$ at each $p$. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} had already frozen the Lewis--Sendov block-diagonal model with $p=0.01$ before the broader benchmark in this chapter. Retaining it therefore provides protocol continuity, not a post-hoc claim that Lewis--Sendov or $p=0.01$ is universally optimal.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:optimisation_conclusion}

Joint reconstruction of the \gls{pet} bed positions is required for the edge-preserving prior to act consistently across their overlap; the qualitative example showed the noise and discontinuities that can remain after separate reconstruction and fusion. For the one-bed convergence study, the prior-application schedule mattered more than the subset organisation: with the average prior weight per data pass matched, only the folded schedule, mathematically identical to evaluating the complete prior at every update, converged, while the sampled and once-per-epoch schedules stagnated under the common preconditioner and step-size protocol. Within the folded schedule, modality-separated subsets converged faster than paired subsets under matched data-projection work, although they also performed twice as many parameter updates and prior-gradient evaluations per pass.

Prior-aware preconditioning was required for stable convergence of the count-limited \gls{pet} reconstruction under the tested shared step schedule. \gls{spect} was more robust, and data-only \gls{bsrem} was sometimes competitive, suggesting that the prior-aware methods can be conservative for that modality. The four prior-curvature models behaved similarly overall, with no universal winner.

This asymmetry is traceable to the prior/data curvature ratio~\eqref{eq:curvature_ratio}, which scales with $\rho_{m,n}^2$ while the data curvature does not depend on $\rho$ at all, so nothing in the present construction keeps that ratio comparable between modalities. A modality-specific curvature weight $\lambda_m$ in the combination~\eqref{eq:modality_precond_weight}, relaxing the prior damping applied to \gls{spect} while leaving the objective untouched, is the targeted remedy and is left as future work. A related methodological point is that the comparisons here use image-domain \gls{nrmse} only, whereas the classical rate results bound the decrease of the objective rather than of the iterate error; objective-gap curves $\mathcal{O}(\bm z^{(k)})-\mathcal{O}^\star$ would connect these experiments to that theory more directly, and would expose monotonicity, which is the observable that distinguishes a majoriser from an approximation.

The Lewis--Sendov Lehmer experiment found only a small advantage for $p=0.01$ over the parallel sum, and that advantage is partly confounded with a small change in effective step size. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} retains the previously frozen Lewis--Sendov block-diagonal/$p=0.01$ configuration for protocol continuity. Its use in that chapter should not be interpreted as selection of the best method after the wider comparison reported here.

\subsection*{Theoretical status of the implemented algorithm}
\label{subsec:theoretical_status}

It is useful to state precisely which guarantees apply and which do not, since the implemented method combines several ingredients whose theories were developed separately.

What is established is the prior side. The objective~\eqref{eq:optimisation_objective} is convex, since the Poisson negative log-likelihood, the $\wdtnv$ prior (Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}) and the non-negativity indicator are all convex. Proposition~\ref{prop:jacobian_majoriser} gives an exact quadratic majoriser of the prior in Jacobian space, whose weight is the matrix analogue of the optimal curvature of a scalar edge-preserving potential, and whose gradient matches that of Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} at the current iterate.

What is not established falls into four identifiable gaps, each attached to a specific ingredient.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Separability.} The majoriser of Proposition~\ref{prop:jacobian_majoriser} is non-separable. The implemented diagonals extract the diagonal rather than applying De Pierro's convexity trick, and so fall short of a majorising diagonal by the factor quantified in Section~\ref{subsubsec:depierro_gap}. Monotonicity is forfeited at this step.
    \item \textbf{Preconditioner class under constraints.} Convergence results for projected gradient methods on the non-negative orthant admit certain diagonal scalings but not general positive-definite preconditioners. The voxel-block methods of Section~\ref{subsubsec:precond_constraint} fall outside that class.
    \item \textbf{Stochasticity.} Classical monotonicity and ordered-subset convergence results assume deterministic gradients or a relaxed deterministic subset schedule. The variance-reduced stochastic estimator~\eqref{eq:svrg_estimator_general} is outside the scope of those results, although the relaxation~\eqref{eq:optimisation_step_size} is of the diminishing-step form that relaxed \gls{bsrem} requires \cite{ahnGloballyConvergentOrdered2002}.
    \item \textbf{Preconditioner staleness.} Monotonicity conditions for preconditioned gradient descent assume a fixed preconditioner. Here $\mathcal{P}^{(k)}$ is refreshed once per epoch and held constant in between, so it is neither fixed nor continuously updated.
\end{enumerate}

No single result in the literature covers this combination, and no monotone-descent guarantee is claimed for the implemented algorithm. Closing gap~1 is the most tractable of the four: it requires only substituting the De Pierro diagonal of~\eqref{eq:depierro_vs_diag} for the diagonal extraction currently used, after which the prior side of the algorithm would carry a majorisation argument end to end.
