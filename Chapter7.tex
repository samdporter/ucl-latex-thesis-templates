\chapter{Optimisation for Synergistic Reconstruction}
\label{chapter:optimisation}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} defined the smoothed weighted directional total nuclear variation prior. This chapter examines how to use that prior in joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction with reliable updates and practical computational cost. Two questions organise the chapter: how data subsets and the prior enter stochastic updates, and how data and prior curvature should scale those updates. The general formulation and design alternatives are introduced first, followed by controlled single-bed phantom experiments comparing their behaviour and cost. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} extends this framework to multi-bed patient acquisitions and evaluates reconstruction quality on phantom and clinical data.

\section{Reconstruction Framework}
\label{sec:optimisation_framework}

Let $\bm z=(z_1,z_2)$ denote the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} images on a shared reconstruction grid. The formulation in Section~\ref{subsec:synergistic_recon} optimised native-grid images $x_m$, with the prior applied to $W_m x_m$. Here we optimise $z_m$ directly, enabling the voxel-wise \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} preconditioners of Section~\ref{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}. The expected data are
\begin{equation}
    \bar y_m(z_m)=A_m W_m^\top z_m+b_m,
    \label{eq:shared_grid_forward_model}
\end{equation}
where $A_m$ is the native-grid system matrix, $b_m$ collects additive corrections, and the discrete adjoint $W_m^\top$ maps shared-grid images to the native projector grid. The data-term gradient is $W_m A_m^\top(\bm 1-y_m/\bar y_m)$, with element-wise division. Thus warping uses $W_m$ and its adjoint; no inverse deformation is estimated. The formulations are not generally equivalent: $z_m$ need not equal $W_m x_m$ for any native image. The reconstructed images are the shared-grid $z_m$; $W_m^\top$ appears only inside the forward model.

The shared grid is the \gls{pet} reconstruction grid, so $W_1$ is the identity. The \gls{spect} warp $W_2$ is estimated by registering its \gls{ctac} image to the \gls{pet} \gls{ctac} before reconstruction and is then held fixed; emission data are not used. It resamples native \gls{spect} images onto the shared grid using non-negative linear interpolation weights and scaling $c_2=(v_1/v_2)^{1/2}$, where $v_m$ is modality $m$'s voxel volume. Its adjoint $W_2^\top$ uses the transposed interpolation weights and the same scaling.

For $S$ subset data functions, define $\mathcal D(\bm z)=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathcal D_s(\bm z)$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(\bm z)
    =
    \mathcal D(\bm z)
    +
    \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}(\bm z)
    +
    \iota_{\mathcal C}(\bm z),
    \label{eq:optimisation_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal C=(\mathbb R_+^N)^2$ enforces $\bm z\geq0$, so all reconstructed voxel values are non-negative. Here $\mathcal{D}_s$ is the Poisson negative log-likelihood for a data subset, including the relevant projector and image-space warping operator, and $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is the prior from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}.

\section{Stochastic \gls{map} Optimisation}
\label{sec:optimisation_svrg}

The stochastic experiments use \gls{svrg} \cite{johnsonAcceleratingStochasticGradient2013,twyman_investigation_2023,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. Recall that the objective~\eqref{eq:optimisation_objective} is $\mathcal O=\mathcal D+\widetilde{\mathcal R}+\iota_{\mathcal C}$, where $\mathcal D$ is the joint Poisson negative log-likelihood, $\widetilde{\mathcal R}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is the smoothed \wdtnv prior of Section~\ref{sec:wdtnv}, and $\iota_{\mathcal C}$ is the indicator of the non-negativity constraint (Section~\ref{sec:recon_basics}). Only the differentiable part $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal D+\widetilde{\mathcal R}$ is split into stochastic components; non-negativity is enforced by projection after every update~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update}. Let $L$ denote the number of sampled components, including any prior-only components, and write them as $F_s$, $s=1,\ldots,L$. Each component list below satisfies $\sum_{s=1}^L F_s=\mathcal F$.

Joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction introduces choices not present in single modality reconstruction: corresponding modality subsets can be paired or treated as separate stochastic functions, and the prior can be scheduled relative to those functions in different ways. The alternatives below comprise two subset organisations and three prior-application schedules motivated by prior subset-selection analysis \cite{porterOptimisingSubsetSelection2024a}.

\subsection{Data-Subset Organisation}
\label{subsec:subset_data_organisation}

Let $N_{\mathrm{PET}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ denote the numbers of data subsets for the two modalities. Two organisations are considered.

\paragraph{Separate subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets are treated as distinct stochastic functions. This gives $S=N_{\mathrm{PET}}+N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \{\mathcal{D}_s\}_{s=1}^{S}
    =
    \{
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},N_{\mathrm{PET}}},
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}}
    \}.
    \label{eq:separate_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Paired subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets with matching indices are combined into one subset. When the modality counts are equal, this gives $S=N_{\mathrm{PET}}=N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{pair}}_s
    =
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},s}
    +
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},s},
    \qquad s=1,\ldots,S.
    \label{eq:paired_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prior-Application Schedules}
\label{subsec:subset_prior_schedules}

For either data organisation, the prior can enter the component list in a number of ways, of which three were investigated. Each list sums to $\mathcal{F}$ and can be used in the \gls{svrg} estimator below.

\paragraph{Folded.}
Here $L=S$ and $F_s=\mathcal D_s+\widetilde{\mathcal R}/S$, $s=1,\ldots,S$. One expected data pass therefore comprises $S$ updates, with the prior gradient difference entering every update.

\paragraph{Prior/half.}
Here $L=2S$: the list contains the $S$ data components and $S$ copies of $\widetilde{\mathcal R}/S$. Uniform sampling selects a prior-only component with probability $1/2$, so an expected data pass comprises $2S$ updates: $S$ data draws and $S$ prior-only draws in expectation.

\paragraph{Once per epoch, on average.}
Here $L=S+1$: one full-prior component $\widetilde{\mathcal R}$ is appended to the $S$ data components. Uniform sampling gives $S+1$ updates per expected data pass, comprising $S$ data draws and one prior-only draw in expectation.

\subsection{SVRG Updates}
\label{subsec:optimisation_svrg_updates}

Sampling uniformly with replacement from the selected component list gives the \gls{svrg} estimator~\eqref{eq:SVRG1}, now taken over the $L$ components of $\mathcal{F}$ at snapshot $\widetilde{\bm z}$,
\[
    g^{(k)}
    =
    \nabla\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    +L\!\left[
        \nabla F_{s_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla F_{s_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad s_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,L\},
\]
%\SA{Compare this to eq\ref{eq:SVRG1} and remind the reader that $\widetilde{\bm z}$ is a "snapshot" (is that the same as "anchor point" ? ) Will do. Yes- the same as anchor point. Do I use both!? Whoops}
so $\mathbb E[g^{(k)}]=\nabla\mathcal{F}(\bm z^{(k)})$. The full-gradient snapshot is refreshed once per epoch.

For the folded schedule, this reduces to
\begin{equation}
    g^{(k)} = \nabla\widetilde{\mathcal R}(\bm z^{(k)})
    + \nabla\mathcal D(\widetilde{\bm z})
    + S\!\left[
        \nabla\mathcal D_{s_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla\mathcal D_{s_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad s_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,S\}.
    \label{eq:svrg_estimator_general}
\end{equation}

The image update is a projected, preconditioned gradient step,
\begin{equation}
    \bm z^{(k+1)}
    =
    \left\{
    \bm z^{(k)}
    -
    \varsigma^{(k)} \mathcal{P}^{(k)} g^{(k)}
    \right\}_{\geq 0},
    \label{eq:optimisation_projected_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}^{(k)}$ is a preconditioner that accounts for local curvature. The step size is iteration dependent and follows the decaying relaxation rule
\begin{equation}
    \varsigma^{(k)}
    =
    \frac{\varsigma^{(0)}}{1+\eta_{\mathrm{relax}} k},
    \label{eq:optimisation_step_size}
\end{equation}
Here $\varsigma^{(0)}$ is the initial step size and $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}$ controls its decay; their experimental values are specified in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}.

\section{Prior-Aware Preconditioning}
\label{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}
\label{subsec:preconditioning}

Preconditioning scales the gradient using an approximation to the inverse local curvature of the smooth objective $\mathcal F$. It can materially affect \gls{map} reconstruction because the data term and the edge-preserving prior can have very different local curvature scales \cite{clinthornePreconditioningMethodsImproved1993,ahn_globally_2003,depierroFastEMlikeMethods2001,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. There are two independent choices: which prior-curvature model to use, and how to combine its preconditioner with the data preconditioner. The comparison below includes data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling, two prior-curvature models for $\wdtnv$ that differ in whether they retain the \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} coupling within each voxel, and inverse-sum and Lehmer combination rules.

\subsection{Data Curvature and BSREM Scaling}
\label{subsec:optimisation_data_preconditioning}

For modality $m$, let $s_m$ denote the sensitivity image on the shared grid,
\begin{equation}
    s_m
    =
    W_m A_m^\top \bm 1.
    \label{eq:shared_grid_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
The data-only \gls{em}/\gls{bsrem} preconditioner is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D,m}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left((z_m+\epsilon_x)\oslash s_m\right),
    \label{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
where $\oslash$ denotes element-wise division and $\operatorname{diag}$ forms the diagonal matrix from the resulting voxel-wise scaling values. Here, $\epsilon_x>0$ is a small image stabiliser heuristically chosen to maintain stability whilst allowing zero-valued voxels to be updated. Its subscript identifies image stabilisation, and distinguishes it from the prior-smoothing parameter $\epsilon$. Equivalently, the corresponding diagonal data Hessian surrogate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal D,m}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left(s_m \oslash (z_m+\epsilon_x)\right).
    \label{eq:data_hessian_surrogate}
\end{equation}
The implementation used in this chapter optionally smooths the numerator image used in the \gls{em} preconditioner with a Gaussian filter, as suggested in \cite{ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}.

\subsection{Prior Curvature Models}
\label{subsec:optimisation_prior_preconditioning}

Prior curvature measures how the prior gradient changes when the images change. Large curvature calls for a smaller update scale, while cross-modal curvature describes how a perturbation of one modality changes the prior gradient for the other. Both models below start from the exact Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty and discard curvature between different voxels; they differ in whether the \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} curvature within each voxel is retained.

Equation~\eqref{eq:phi_gram_spectral} expresses the penalty as a function of the eigenvalues of the symmetric Gram matrix $\mathcal{G}^\top \mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{G}$ denotes the local weighted directional Jacobian with its voxel index and image dependence suppressed. The Lewis--Sendov formula \cite{doi:10.1137/S089547980036838X}, followed by the chain rule through $\mathcal{G}\mapsto \mathcal{G}^\top \mathcal{G}$, gives its local Hessian. An alternative family, built from a quadratic majoriser of the prior rather than its Hessian, is described in Appendix~\ref{app:mm_preconditioner}.

\subsubsection{Voxel-block curvature}
\label{subsubsec:block_curvature_model}

At each voxel, a $2\times2$ matrix describes how the prior affects the PET and SPECT updates. Its diagonal entries describe each modality separately, while its off-diagonal entries describe their coupling. Writing $H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}=\nabla^2\widetilde{\mathcal R}$ for the full image-space Hessian, the voxel-block approximation is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}block}}
    =
    \operatorname{blkdiag}
    \left(
        \widehat H_{11}^{\mathrm{LS}},\ldots,\widehat H_{NN}^{\mathrm{LS}}
    \right),
    \qquad
    \widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\approx[H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}]_{nn}\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}.
    \label{eq:ls_block_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
The local derivatives are exact, but their assembly into voxel blocks is approximate, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:ls_block_assembly}. The blocks are positive semidefinite.

\subsubsection{Diagonal curvature}
\label{subsubsec:diag_curvature_model}

The diagonal model keeps only the diagonal entries of each voxel block,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R},n}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}diag}}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\!\left(
        \bigl[\widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\bigr]_{11},\,
        \bigl[\widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\bigr]_{22}
    \right),
    \label{eq:ls_diag_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
and so discards the cross-modal curvature. These entries are non-negative because each block is positive semidefinite.

\subsection{Combining Data and Prior Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}

At each voxel, the data-curvature block is $\operatorname{diag}([\widehat H_{\mathcal D,1}]_{nn},[\widehat H_{\mathcal D,2}]_{nn})$, paired with the selected prior block. Suppressing the voxel index, let $\mathcal P_{\mathcal D}$ and $\mathcal P_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}$ denote the component preconditioners after the numerical stabilisation in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_implementation}; in the formulas below, $\widehat H_{\mathcal D}=\mathcal P_{\mathcal D}^{-1}$ and $\widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}=\mathcal P_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{-1}$.

\subsubsection{Inverse-sum combination}

The curvatures of the data term and prior add. This motivates adding their curvature surrogates before inverting the sum. The inverse-sum, or parallel-sum, combination \cite{ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a} is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{P}_{\parallel}
        &=
        \left(\widehat H_{\mathcal D}+\widehat H_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}\right)^{-1}
        \\
        &=
        \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:parallel_sum_combination}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Lehmer extension}
\label{subsec:optimisation_lehmer}

The inverse sum is dominated by the larger of the two curvatures, so a very large data or prior curvature produces a small update scale. The Lehmer family extends the inverse sum with an order parameter $p$ that can relax this scaling. For positive scalar data and prior preconditioner entries $d$ and $r$, the unscaled Lehmer mean is
\[
    L_p(d,r)
    =\frac{d^p+r^p}{d^{p-1}+r^{p-1}}
    =d f_p(r/d),
    \qquad
    f_p(t)=\frac{t^p+1}{t^{p-1}+1}.
\]
Setting $t=r/d$, its $p=0$ case gives
\[
    \tfrac12 d f_0(r/d)
    =
    \tfrac12 d\frac{2(r/d)}{1+r/d}
    =
    \frac{dr}{d+r}
    =
    (d^{-1}+r^{-1})^{-1}.
\]
At $p=0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$, $L_p$ is the harmonic, geometric and arithmetic mean of $d$ and $r$, so increasing $p$ gives the larger entry more influence.

For the symmetric positive-definite $2\times2$ preconditioners at each voxel, the matrix extension is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        T
        &=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1/2}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{-1/2},
        \\
        L_p(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D},\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal R}})
        &=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{1/2}f_p(T)\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal D}^{1/2}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:matrix_lehmer_combination}
\end{equation}
Here $T$ is symmetric positive definite and expresses the prior preconditioner relative to the data preconditioner. The function is applied spectrally: if $T=U\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2)U^\top$, where $U$ is orthogonal and $t_i>0$, then
\[
    f_p(T)
    =
    U\operatorname{diag}\!\left(f_p(t_1),f_p(t_2)\right)U^\top.
\]

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_implementation}

Before inversion, curvature eigenvalues below $10^{-8}$ are raised to $10^{-8}$. Before combination, eigenvalues below $10^{-6}$ in both component preconditioners are raised to $10^{-6}$. Computing the local derivatives requires voxel-wise singular-value decompositions, so the prior blocks are refreshed once per epoch and held fixed between refreshes. They therefore use curvature evaluated at the start of the epoch. Because the preconditioner keeps changing between epochs, the usual convergence guarantees for fixed-preconditioner methods do not apply. They could be recovered by freezing the preconditioner after a fixed number of epochs, but we did not do this.

After each update~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update}, negative voxel values are set to zero. When the data and prior curvature models are diagonal, the combined preconditioner is diagonal, so component-wise clipping is the compatible projection. With coupled PET/SPECT blocks, this clipping can spoil a direction that would otherwise reduce the objective, so the usual projected-gradient guarantees do not apply. We did not identify a failure attributable to clipping in these experiments, although its effect was not tested separately.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:optimisation_experiments}

The experiments compare the alternatives defined above while keeping the reconstruction objective fixed. Subset scheduling is assessed with one curvature model; with one subset schedule then fixed, the prior-curvature model is varied first and the combination rule second. These are controlled comparisons, not a joint search over all settings.

\subsection{Common Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}

The optimisation experiments used an anthropomorphic phantom scanned on the Mediso AnyScan at the National Physical Laboratory. A single \gls{pet} bed position covered all activity, although inactive parts of the phantom extended beyond the axial field of view. For this rigid phantom, $W_2$ used a single rigid transformation estimated by symmetric block matching \cite{modatGlobalImageRegistration2014}. \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} were each partitioned into 18 subsets. Each modality was initialised from a uniform image of ones by 10 epochs of \gls{osem}, with the same 18 subsets, followed by Gaussian smoothing with $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ \gls{fwhm} in each direction.Each candidate configuration was evaluated over 20 expected data passes with five stochastic realisations and initial step $\varsigma^{(0)}=1$. The 20-epoch folded, modality-separated runs used $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$; the subset experiment rescales this rate to match relaxation across schedules. A single 1000-epoch reconstruction of the same objective, using a diagonal preconditioner and slower relaxation decay ($\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=10^{-4}$), provided the reference image pair $\bm z^\star$. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_convergence} reports the objective and iterate diagnostics supporting its effective stationarity relative to the best observed iterate.

For each stochastic run and each saved iteration, the \acrfull{nrmse} is evaluated separately for the shared-grid modality images $z_m^{(k)}$ and references $z_m^\star$, with $m=1$ for \gls{pet} and $m=2$ for \gls{spect}. In a shared-grid region $R$, it is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{NRMSE}(z_m^{(k)},z_m^\star;R)
    =
    \frac{\sqrt{|R|^{-1}\sum_{n\in R}(z^{(k)}_{m,n}-z^\star_{m,n})^2}}
         {\sqrt{|R|^{-1}\sum_{n\in R}(z^\star_{m,n})^2}}.
    \label{eq:optimisation_nrmse}
\end{equation}
The regions are evaluated separately over the whole image, the hot-sphere mask, the cold-sphere mask, and the liver compartment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_voi_overlay}. The whole-image region includes the low-activity background surrounding the phantom, and is therefore the most sensitive of the four to behaviour in voxels that carry no clinical information; the liver is the largest structure of interest and the region over which absorbed dose to normal tissue would be scored. The plotted centre line is the arithmetic mean of the five per-run \gls{nrmse}; the envelope is their pointwise minimum--maximum.

\label{sec:optimisation_subset_selection}

The first experiment asks how subset organisation and prior scheduling affect progress towards the reference. It crosses the two data organisations with the three prior schedules, holding the curvature model fixed.

\subsubsection{Results and interpretation}

Figure~\ref{fig:optim_subset_organisation} separates the six configurations mainly by prior schedule. The folded runs reduce mean \gls{nrmse} over the tested 20-pass interval; separate-folded attains the lowest mean \gls{nrmse} at pass 20 in every region for both modalities, with paired-folded close behind. The separate-folded \gls{pet} whole-image \gls{nrmse} curve has a transient jump near pass six before recovering. Neither the sampled nor once-per-epoch schedule shows a net \gls{nrmse} reduction over the same interval.

All three schedules give the same prior update on average. Folded scheduling applies it at every update, whereas the other schedules apply larger updates less frequently. These intermittent  updates may contribute to the poorer behaviour observed here, although the experiment also changes how data components are sampled.

The step size was matched at each expected data pass, so different relaxation rates do not explain the result. 

\subsection{Block versus Diagonal Preconditioning}
\label{subsec:optimisation_block_vs_diag}

The next experiment asks whether incorporating prior curvature improves the updates, and whether retaining within-voxel coupling improves them further. It holds the subset organisation and prior schedule fixed while changing the preconditioner.

\subsubsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{subsec:preconditioner_experiment_methods}

The comparison uses modality-separated subsets and the folded prior schedule, which gave the lowest final mean \gls{nrmse} in the subset experiment. All methods use the common protocol above, with the same objective, initialisation and long-run reference. Three preconditioners are compared: data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling~\eqref{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}, and its inverse-sum combinations~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} with the block~\eqref{eq:ls_block_preconditioner} and diagonal~\eqref{eq:ls_diag_preconditioner} prior models.

The prior-aware preconditioners follow the numerical stabilisation and epoch refresh procedure described in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_implementation}.

All methods minimise the same objective, so the long-run reconstruction provides a common reference $\bm z^\star$. Agreement with this reference is assessed experimentally; it is not a convergence guarantee.

It uses the common evaluation protocol and initial step $\varsigma^{(0)}=1$.

In Figure~\ref{fig:optim_lehmer_parallel}, the order $p=0.1$ is clearly worse than the inverse sum ($p=0$) in every region of both modalities. The order $p=0.01$ is close to the inverse sum. For \gls{pet} it is as good or marginally better in every region, including the hot sphere, and avoids the transient whole-image increase near pass 14, although it shows a smaller one near pass 8. For \gls{spect} it is slightly worse in the hot and cold spheres and slightly better in the liver.

Changing $p$ also changes the effective step scale. The factor $\tfrac12$ is calibrated at $p=0$; for eigenvalues of $T$ of order $10^2$, $p=0.01$ increases the spectral factor by roughly two per cent relative to the inverse sum, rising to a few per cent at $10^3$. A fixed-$\varsigma^{(0)}$ sweep therefore mixes the effect of the Lehmer order with a modest step-size change. Since $p=0.01$ was as good as or marginally better than the inverse sum for \gls{pet}, including the hot sphere, it was retained for Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:optimisation_discussion}

The block and diagonal models for the preconditioners behaved similarly. Two mechanisms plausibly limit the benefit of retaining the within-voxel coupling. First, cross-modal curvature is appreciable only where both modalities carry non-negligible local gradients, which may be a small fraction of a volumetric \gls{voi}. Second, wherever the data curvature dominates the inverse sum~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination}, differences between prior models are attenuated. Neither effect was measured.

The modality dependence in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_preconditioners} plausibly reflects the balance between data and prior curvature. The modality weights $\rho_{m,n}$ were chosen in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} to balance the gradients entering the nuclear norm, independently of the modality sensitivities, so the prior/data curvature ratio can differ between \gls{pet} and \gls{spect}. A separate modality scaling of the prior curvature in the preconditioner could change the convergence rate without changing the objective; it was not investigated here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:optimisation_conclusion}

In this single-bed phantom subset experiment, all schedules used the same smooth objective and an unbiased uniform-sampling \gls{svrg} estimator. We rescaled the relaxation rate from the folded, modality-separated setting $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$, giving every configuration the same step size at a given expected data pass. On that expected data-exposure axis, the folded schedules reduced \gls{nrmse} over 20 expected data passes and the intermittent schedules did not. Since the total update counts differ, this comparison does not establish a wall-clock or compute-efficiency advantage.

Prior-aware preconditioning kept count-limited \gls{pet} bounded over the same 20-pass interval, where data-only \gls{bsrem} fails to decrease the distance with the converged image within 20 passes; for \gls{spect}, data-only scaling remained competitive. Retaining the within-voxel \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} curvature gave modest \gls{pet} gains over the diagonal model. The Lehmer order $p=0.01$ gave similarly modest \gls{pet} gains over the inverse sum, whereas $p=0.1$ was clearly worse. The block model's modest \gls{pet} gains led to its use in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} with $p=0.01$.

These single-bed experiments used rigid registration. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} extends the shared-grid formulation to two overlapping \gls{pet} bed positions and deformable patient registration.
