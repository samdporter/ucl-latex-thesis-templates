\chapter{Optimisation for Synergistic Reconstruction}
\label{chapter:optimisation}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} defined the smoothed weighted directional total nuclear variation prior. This chapter examines how to use that prior in joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction with reliable updates and practical computational cost. Three questions organise the chapter: how overlapping \gls{pet} beds contribute to a single activity field, how data subsets and the prior enter stochastic updates, and how data and prior curvature should scale those updates. The general formulation and design alternatives are introduced first, followed by controlled one-bed phantom experiments comparing their behaviour and cost.

\section{Reconstruction Framework}
\label{sec:optimisation_framework}

Let $\bm z=(z_1,z_2)$ again denote the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} images represented on a common reconstruction grid. For $N_{\mathrm d}$ subset data functions, define $\mathcal D(\bm z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm d}}\mathcal D_i(\bm z)$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(\bm z)
    =
    \mathcal D(\bm z)
    +
    \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}(\bm z)
    +
    \iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}(\bm z),
    \label{eq:optimisation_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_i$ is a Poisson negative log-likelihood term composed with the relevant projector and image-space registration operator; $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$ is the prior from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}; and $\iota_{\mathbb{R}_+}$ is the indicator of the non-negative orthant, equal to zero when $\bm z\geq 0$ component-wise and $+\infty$ otherwise.

\subsection{Multi-Bed \gls{pet} Acquisition}
\label{sec:multibed}

Whole-liver \gls{pet} commonly requires multiple partially overlapping bed positions because a single axial field of view does not cover the organ. Structures of interest can therefore extend into the low-sensitivity edge of an individual bed, while two beds contribute measurements of the shared anatomy in their overlap. A coupled edge-preserving prior ideally should act on one activity field spanning the whole acquisition, rather than on images divided by scanner bed boundaries.

\subsubsection{Why Not Stitch Independent Reconstructions?}
\label{subsec:stitching_failure}

A common practical alternative is to reconstruct each \gls{pet} bed independently and fuse the images using sensitivity-weighted stitching in the overlap. With an edge-preserving prior, however, each reconstruction makes its own noise-suppression and edge decisions before fusion. The qualitative example in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_multibed_qualitative} shows overlap-region noise and discontinuities after separate reconstruction and fusion. It is included to motivate the joint formulation; no quantitative comparison was performed \cite{porterSimultaneousMultiBedMAP2025}.

\subsubsection{Joint Multi-Bed Formulation}
\label{subsec:multibed_notation}
\label{subsec:joint_multibed}

Let $x$ be the \gls{pet} activity image on the combined reconstruction grid. For bed position $\mathrm{bed}=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{bed}}$, let $R_{\mathrm{bed}}$ restrict the combined image to the axial support reconstructed by that bed. The expected \gls{pet} data for bed position $\mathrm{bed}$ are
\begin{equation}
    \bar y_{\mathrm{bed}}(x)
    =
    A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}},
    \label{eq:multibed_forward_model}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{bed}}$ is the bed-specific \gls{pet} system matrix and $b_{\mathrm{bed}}$ collects additive corrections. The joint \gls{pet} data term is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}(x)
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \left(
        y_{\mathrm{bed}} \mid A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multibed_data_term}
\end{equation}
with gradient
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}(x)
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    R_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top
    A_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top
    \nabla_{\bar y_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \left(
        y_{\mathrm{bed}} \mid A_{\mathrm{bed}} R_{\mathrm{bed}} x + b_{\mathrm{bed}}
    \right).
    \label{eq:multibed_gradient}
\end{equation}
The corresponding \gls{pet} sensitivity image on the combined grid is
\begin{equation}
    s_{\mathrm{PET}}^{\mathrm{multi}}
    =
    \sum_{\mathrm{bed}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bed}}}
    R_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top A_{\mathrm{bed}}^\top \bm 1.
    \label{eq:multibed_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Each bed thus contributes its own data term and sensitivity to a single combined image, while the image-domain prior is evaluated over the complete field of view. The formulation is needed for the dual-bed application in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper}. The controlled subset and preconditioner studies below use a one-bed phantom to isolate optimisation effects.

\section{Stochastic \gls{map} Optimisation}
\label{sec:optimisation_svrg}

Section \ref{sec:optimisation} introduced stochastic optimisation as a promising method for fast and convergent image reconstruction. The stochastic experiments in this chapter use \gls{svrg} \cite{johnsonAcceleratingStochasticGradient2013,twyman_investigation_2023,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. Writing $\mathcal R=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{w\text{-}dTNV}}$, define the smooth objective $\Phi=\mathcal D+\mathcal R$. A component list $\{F_j\}_{j=1}^{n}$ satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_j=\Phi$ is sampled uniformly with replacement, giving the CIL \gls{svrg} estimator at anchor point $\widetilde{\bm z}$,
\[
    g^{(k)}
    =
    \nabla\Phi(\widetilde{\bm z})
    +n\!\left[
        \nabla F_{j_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla F_{j_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad j_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,n\},
\]
so $\mathbb E[g^{(k)}]=\nabla\Phi(\bm z^{(k)})$. The full-gradient snapshot is refreshed once per epoch.

The image update is a projected, preconditioned gradient step,
\begin{equation}
    \bm z^{(k+1)}
    =
    \left\{
    \bm z^{(k)}
    -
    \varsigma^{(k)} \mathcal{P}^{(k)} g^{(k)}
    \right\}_{\geq 0},
    \label{eq:optimisation_projected_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}^{(k)}$ is either a diagonal preconditioner or a voxel-wise $2\times2$ block preconditioner\footnote{For a non-diagonal preconditioner, component-wise projection onto the non-negative orthant may destroy the descent property of the preconditioned update. Section~\ref{subsubsec:precond_constraint} explains this limitation.} coupling the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} update components. The step size is iteration dependent and follows the decaying relaxation rule
\begin{equation}
    \varsigma^{(k)}
    =
    \frac{\varsigma^{(0)}}{1+\eta_{\mathrm{relax}} k},
    \label{eq:optimisation_step_size}
\end{equation}
Here $\varsigma^{(0)}$ is the initial step size and $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}$ controls its decay; their experimental values are specified in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}.

Subset methods accelerate early iterations in emission tomography by replacing full data passes with stochastic or ordered blocks of projection data \cite{hudsonAcceleratedImageReconstruction1994}. Joint \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} reconstruction introduces two further choices: corresponding modality subsets can be paired or treated as separate stochastic functions, and the prior can be scheduled relative to those functions in different ways. The alternatives below comprise two subset organisations and three prior-application schedules motivated by prior subset-selection analysis \cite{porterOptimisingSubsetSelection2024a}.

\subsection{Data-Subset Organisation}
\label{subsec:subset_data_organisation}

Let $N_{\mathrm{PET}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ denote the numbers of data subsets for the two modalities. Two organisations are considered.

\paragraph{Separate subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets are treated as distinct stochastic functions. This gives $N_{\mathrm d}=N_{\mathrm{PET}}+N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \{\mathcal{D}_i\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm d}}
    =
    \{
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},N_{\mathrm{PET}}},
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}}
    \}.
    \label{eq:separate_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Paired subsets.}
\gls{pet} and \gls{spect} subsets with matching indices are summed into one stochastic function. When $N_{\mathrm{PET}}=N_{\mathrm{SPECT}}=N$, this gives $N_{\mathrm d}=N$ functions,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{pair}}_j
    =
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{PET},j}
    +
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPECT},j},
    \qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
    \label{eq:paired_subset_functions}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prior-Application Schedules}
\label{subsec:subset_prior_schedules}

For either data organisation, the prior can enter the component list in many ways, of which three were investigated. Each list sums to $\Phi$ and therefore preserves the unbiasedness of the estimator above.

\paragraph{Folded.}
Here $n=N_{\mathrm d}$ and $F_i=\mathcal D_i+\mathcal R/N_{\mathrm d}$. One expected data pass therefore comprises $N_{\mathrm d}$ updates, with the prior gradient difference entering every update. Expanding the general estimator gives
\begin{equation}
    g^{(k)} = \nabla\mathcal R(\bm z^{(k)})
    + \nabla\mathcal D(\widetilde{\bm z})
    + N_{\mathrm d}\!\left[
        \nabla\mathcal D_{i_k}(\bm z^{(k)})
        -\nabla\mathcal D_{i_k}(\widetilde{\bm z})
    \right],
    \qquad i_k\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm d}\}.
    \label{eq:svrg_estimator_general}
\end{equation}
Thus the folded schedule is equivalent to keeping the complete prior outside the sampler and evaluating its gradient at every update.

\paragraph{Sampled.}
Here $n=2N_{\mathrm d}$: the list contains the $N_{\mathrm d}$ data components and $N_{\mathrm d}$ copies of $\mathcal R/N_{\mathrm d}$. Uniform sampling selects a prior-only component with probability $1/2$, so an expected data pass comprises $2N_{\mathrm d}$ updates: $N_{\mathrm d}$ data draws and $N_{\mathrm d}$ prior-only draws in expectation. A prior draw contributes twice the prior gradient difference. This schedule is labelled prior/half in the figure legends.

\paragraph{Once per epoch.}
Here $n=N_{\mathrm d}+1$: one full-prior component $\mathcal R$ is appended to the $N_{\mathrm d}$ data components. Uniform sampling gives $N_{\mathrm d}+1$ updates per expected data pass, comprising $N_{\mathrm d}$ data draws and one prior-only draw in expectation. Thus the label denotes an average frequency, not a deterministic dedicated update; a prior draw contributes $(N_{\mathrm d}+1)$ times the prior gradient difference. This inverse-probability scaling is required for unbiasedness, but concentrates the prior correction into comparatively large individual updates.

\section{Prior-Aware Preconditioning}
\label{sec:optimisation_preconditioners}
\label{subsec:preconditioning}

Preconditioning can materially affect \gls{map} reconstruction because the data term and the edge-preserving prior can have very different local curvature scales \cite{clinthornePreconditioningMethodsImproved1993,ahn_globally_2003,depierroFastEMlikeMethods2001,ehrhardtFastPETReconstruction2025a}. The comparison below includes data-only \gls{bsrem} scaling and four prior-aware curvature models for $\wdtnv$.

\subsection{Data Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_data_preconditioning}

For modality $m$, let $s_m$ denote the sensitivity image on the shared grid,
\begin{equation}
    s_m
    =
    W_m A_m^\top \bm 1,
    \label{eq:shared_grid_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $A_m$ is the native-grid system matrix and $W_m$ maps native images to the shared prior grid. The data-only \gls{em}/\gls{bsrem} preconditioner is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{BSREM},m}
    =
    (z_m+\epsilon_x)\oslash s_m,
    \label{eq:bsrem_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
with element-wise division. This scaling models the data term only; it contains no prior curvature. Equivalently, the corresponding diagonal data Hessian surrogate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathrm{data},m}
    =
    s_m \oslash (z_m+\epsilon_x).
    \label{eq:data_hessian_surrogate}
\end{equation}
The implementation used in this chapter optionally smooths the numerator image used in the \gls{em} preconditioner with a Gaussian filter.

\subsection{Combining Data and Prior Curvature}
\label{subsec:optimisation_preconditioner_combination}

Let $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}=\widehat H_{\mathrm D}^{-1}$ denote the data preconditioner and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}=\widehat H_{\mathcal R}^{-1}$ a prior preconditioner whose curvature model is developed below. For block methods, $\widehat H_{\mathrm D}$ is the $2\times2$ diagonal matrix containing the modality-specific data curvatures at each voxel. The principal comparison combines the operands through the inverse-sum, or parallel-sum, rule
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\parallel}
    =
    \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
    =
    \left(\widehat H_{\mathrm D}+\widehat H_{\mathcal R}\right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:parallel_sum_combination}
\end{equation}
The same expression applies to diagonal and voxel-block operands, with numerical floors used before inversion.

The \gls{mm} interpretation of~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} is conditional. If valid data and prior majorisers are constructed at a common anchor, their curvatures add, and the inverse of $\widehat H_{\mathrm D}+\widehat H_{\mathcal R}$ is the corresponding \gls{mm} preconditioner. Equation~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination} always adds the selected curvature models, but a global \gls{mm} interpretation follows only when both operands satisfy that condition. None of the implemented diagonal or voxel-block reductions is established here as a global majoriser.

\subsection{Prior Curvature Models}
\label{subsec:optimisation_prior_preconditioning}

Four prior-curvature constructions were considered: three based on a local \acrfull{mm}/\acrfull{irls} surrogate and one based on the Lewis--Sendov Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty. They differ along two axes: the curvature source (the frozen \glsentryshort{mm} surrogate or the exact smoothed spectral Hessian) and the treatment of within-voxel \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} coupling (dropped, bounded, or retained). All four discard curvature between different voxels.

\subsubsection{A quadratic majoriser in Jacobian space}
\label{subsubsec:jacobian_majoriser}

Let $Y,Y_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}$ with $d\geq M$, let $g$ be the Charbonnier function of Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv}, let $\phi(Y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}g(\sigma_\ell(Y))$ and write
\begin{equation}
    C_0 := \Omegaeps{Y_0} = \left(Y_0^\top Y_0 + \epsilon^2\mathbb{I}_M\right)^{-1/2}
    \label{eq:majoriser_weight}
\end{equation}
for the weight evaluated at the current point. Then, for all $Y$,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(Y)
    \;\leq\;
    \phi(Y_0)
    +\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(Y^\top Y - Y_0^\top Y_0\right)C_0\right],
    \label{eq:jacobian_majoriser}
\end{equation}
with equality at $Y=Y_0$.

The bound is the matrix form of the scalar construction in Figure~\ref{fig:majoriser_change_of_variable}. Although the Charbonnier potential $\psi(s)=\sqrt{s^2+\epsilon^2}-\epsilon$ is convex in $s$, it is concave in $s^2$. Its tangent in the variable $s^2$ is therefore an upper bound and becomes a quadratic in $s$. The matrix result follows by replacing $s^2$ with the Gram matrix $Y^\top Y$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/preconditioning/majoriser_change_of_variable.png}
    \caption{Scalar form of the quadratic majoriser. In the variable $a=s^2$, the Charbonnier potential is concave and its tangent maps back to a quadratic upper bound in $s$. Here $\epsilon=0.3$ and $s_0=1.2$.}
    \label{fig:majoriser_change_of_variable}
\end{figure}

The eigenvalues of $Y^\top Y$ are $\sigma_\ell(Y)^2$ for $\ell=1,\dots,M$, so
\begin{equation}
    \phi(Y)
    =\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\left(\sqrt{\sigma_\ell(Y)^2+\epsilon^2}-\epsilon\right)
    =\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\left(Y^\top Y+\epsilon^2\mathbb{I}_M\right)^{1/2}\right)-M\epsilon ,
    \label{eq:phi_as_trace}
\end{equation}
Writing $A=Y^\top Y$ therefore gives $\phi(Y)=f(A)$, where $f(A)=\operatorname{tr}((A+\epsilon^2\mathbb{I}_M)^{1/2})-M\epsilon$. The matrix square root is operator concave on the positive semidefinite cone \cite{bhatiaMatrixAnalysis1997a}, so $f$ is concave and obeys the tangent bound
\begin{equation}
    f(A)\;\leq\;f(A_0)+\operatorname{tr}\!\left[(A-A_0)\,\nabla f(A_0)\right],
    \qquad
    \nabla f(A_0)=\tfrac12 C_0 ,
    \label{eq:concave_tangent}
\end{equation}
and substituting $A=Y^\top Y$ and $A_0=Y_0^\top Y_0$ gives~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser}. Since $C_0\succ0$, its right-hand side is a convex quadratic in $Y$ that touches $\phi$ at $Y_0$ and has gradient $Y_0C_0$ there, matching Equation~\eqref{eq:2x2_gram}. For $M=1$, $C_0=(s_0^2+\epsilon^2)^{-1/2}$ is the familiar half-quadratic or \gls{irls} weight for the Charbonnier potential \cite{fessler_image_2017, charbonnier_deterministic_1997}; Equation~\eqref{eq:majoriser_weight} is its spectral generalisation.

Summing~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser} over voxels gives a frozen-anchor quadratic majoriser of the complete prior. Its Hessian has the form $\bm{D}^\ast(\cdot)\bm{D}$, where $\bm{D}$ collects the weighted directional finite-difference operators $\rho_{m,n}\Pi_{m,n}D$. This result is exact but spatially non-separable: neighbouring voxels remain coupled. The implemented models below reduce this curvature to diagonal or voxel-block form.

For the \gls{mm} family, let $k_0$ denote a preconditioner-refresh iteration and, at voxel $n$, define the anchored weighted directional Jacobian
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n^{(k_0)}
    :=
    \tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n\!\left(\bm z^{(k_0)}\right)
    =
    \left[
        \rho_{1,n}\Pi_{1,n}(Dz_1^{(k_0)})_n
        \;\middle|\;
        \rho_{2,n}\Pi_{2,n}(Dz_2^{(k_0)})_n
    \right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}
    \label{eq:optimisation_weighted_jacobian}
\end{equation}
This is the weighted directional Jacobian from Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} (Equation~\eqref{eq:Yn_weighted}). At each preconditioner refresh, the modality-space \glsentryshort{mm} weight is
\begin{equation}
    C_n^{(k_0)}
    =
    \Omegaeps{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_n^{(k_0)}}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        c_{11,n}^{(k_0)} & c_{12,n}^{(k_0)}\\
        c_{12,n}^{(k_0)} & c_{22,n}^{(k_0)}
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:mm_weight_current_code}
\end{equation}
This is the spectral weight~\eqref{eq:spectral_weight}, evaluated at the refresh anchor and then held fixed until the next refresh. Below, the superscript is suppressed: $C_n$ and $c_{mm',n}$ denote these anchored quantities.

\subsubsection{Voxel-block curvature model}
\label{subsubsec:block_curvature_model}

The least severe reduction of the frozen curvature retains, at each voxel, the full $2\times2$ modality block coupling the \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} update components. Let $\mathcal{N}(n)$ be the target voxels whose finite-difference stencil contains source voxel $n$, and let $c_{r\leftarrow n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the difference-channel impulse vector: the response at target $r$ of the boundary-adjusted discrete directional-difference operator to a unit impulse at source voxel $n$. Its entries carry the channel scales $s_{m,r,\ell}$ returned by the implemented discrete-Jacobian sensitivity, which include the inverse physical step length and the normalisation of the chosen stencil bank. For the shared six-neighbour, unit-step study stencil these scales are identical for the two modalities, so a single impulse vector serves both factors of the cross-modal term in Equation~\eqref{eq:block_alpha}; a stencil bank with modality-dependent coefficients would instead require modality-indexed vectors $c^{(m)}_{r\leftarrow n}$ in each factor. The voxel-block curvature uses
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{mm'}
    =
    c_{r\leftarrow n}^\top
    \Pi_{m,r}^\top
    \Pi_{m',r}
    c_{r\leftarrow n},
    \label{eq:block_alpha}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,n}^{\mathrm{loc\text{-}block}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{r\in\mathcal{N}(n)}
    \left[
        B_r C_r B_r
    \right]
    \circ
    A_{r\leftarrow n},
    \qquad
    A_{r\leftarrow n}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{11} & \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{12}\\
        \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{21} & \alpha_{r\leftarrow n}^{22}
    \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:block_tight_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
where $B_r=\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{1,r},\rho_{2,r})$ and $\circ$ denotes the Hadamard product.
Equivalently, its individual modality entries are
\begin{equation}
    \left[\widehat H_{\mathcal R,n}^{\mathrm{loc\text{-}block}}\right]_{mm'}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{r\in\mathcal{N}(n)}
    \rho_{m,r}\rho_{m',r}\,
    c_{mm',r}\,
    c_{r\leftarrow n}^{\top}
    \Pi_{m,r}^{\top}\Pi_{m',r}
    c_{r\leftarrow n}.
    \label{eq:block_tight_preconditioner_entries}
\end{equation}
For $m\neq m'$, Equation~\eqref{eq:block_tight_preconditioner_entries} explicitly retains both the cross-modal spectral weight $c_{12,r}$ and the cross-projector product $\Pi_{1,r}^{\top}\Pi_{2,r}$. The voxel-block reduction therefore discards curvature between different source voxels while retaining the \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} curvature within each voxel. For the shared six-neighbour, unit-step study stencil, the target-dependent accumulation implemented by \texttt{mm\_diag\_block\_tight} gives this exact voxel-diagonal block of the frozen surrogate before symmetrisation and numerical positive-definite flooring.

The accumulation is inexpensive for this stencil. The sum over $\mathcal{N}(n)$ contains at most $d+1$ non-zero terms: the anchor target $r=n$, whose impulse vector collects the coefficient of every forward difference based at $n$, and the $d$ backward neighbours $r=n-\delta_\ell\in\Omega$, whose impulse vectors are non-zero in channel $\ell$ alone. One refresh therefore evaluates one full projector-Gram quadratic form and $d$ shifted single-channel terms per voxel for each modality pair. Performed once per epoch, this cost is negligible relative to a single projection pass; for other stencils supported by the gradient API, the literal boundary-adjusted impulse coefficients remain the defining quantities.

\subsubsection{Diagonal curvature models}

A scalar metric removes the remaining within-voxel coupling by diagonalising each $2\times2$ block, and this can be done in two ways: by extraction or by bounding. The extracted diagonal is the exact scalar (Jacobi) diagonal of the frozen \glsentryshort{mm} surrogate, given by Equation~\eqref{eq:block_tight_preconditioner_entries} at $m=m'$,
\begin{equation}
    \left[H_{\mathcal R}^{\mathrm{frozen}}\right]_{(m,n),(m,n)}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{r\in\mathcal{N}(n)}
    \rho_{m,r}^{2}\,c_{mm,r}\,
    c_{r\leftarrow n}^{\top}
    \Pi_{m,r}^{\top}\Pi_{m,r}
    c_{r\leftarrow n}.
    \label{eq:frozen_surrogate_exact_diagonal}
\end{equation}
Extracting a diagonal as a weighted sum of squared operator coefficients is the standard Jacobi construction for curvature of the form $\mathcal B^\top W\mathcal B$~\cite{fessler_conjugate-gradient_1999, sotthivirat_image_2002}, and Equation~\eqref{eq:frozen_surrogate_exact_diagonal} is independent of the factorisation used to evaluate it; coupled-field Hessians additionally contain the corresponding off-diagonal field blocks~\cite{crestel_comparative_2018}.

The \glsentryshort{mm} Jacobi-diagonal operand, the extraction strategy, uses Equation~\eqref{eq:frozen_surrogate_exact_diagonal} directly,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,m,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}J}}
    =
    \left[H_{\mathcal R}^{\mathrm{frozen}}\right]_{(m,n),(m,n)}.
    \label{eq:diag_tight_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
Its accumulation is the $m=m'$ restriction of the voxel-block accumulation above. Although a scalar diagonal contains $c_{mm,r}$ rather than $c_{12,r}$, $c_{mm,r}$ is obtained from the full coupled matrix $C_r$ and consequently depends on both modalities; the extraction nevertheless discards the off-diagonal block entries without bounding them.

The bounding strategy replaces each anchored weight matrix by its Gershgorin-dominant diagonal, $\widehat C_r=\operatorname{diag}(c_{11,r}+|c_{12,r}|,\,c_{22,r}+|c_{12,r}|)$, which satisfies $\widehat C_r\succeq C_r$ since the difference has eigenvalues $0$ and $2|c_{12,r}|$. The frozen curvature depends on the target weights through a congruence, which preserves the semidefinite order, so this substitution produces a modality-decoupled majoriser of the frozen curvature; the Gershgorin operand is the exact diagonal of that majoriser, evaluated by the same impulse accumulation,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,m,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}G}}
    =
    V_{\mathrm{vox}}
    \sum_{r\in\mathcal{N}(n)}
    \rho_{m,r}^{2}\,
    \left(c_{mm,r}+|c_{12,r}|\right)
    c_{r\leftarrow n}^{\top}
    \Pi_{m,r}^{\top}\Pi_{m,r}
    c_{r\leftarrow n}.
    \label{eq:diag_gersh_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
This diagonal also dominates the voxel block it diagonalises. At each target, the difference $\bigl[B_r(\widehat C_r-C_r)B_r\bigr]\circ A_{r\leftarrow n}$ is the Hadamard product of a positive-semidefinite matrix with the Gram matrix $A_{r\leftarrow n}$, hence positive semidefinite by the Schur product theorem, so before numerical flooring
\begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{diag}\bigl(
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,1,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}G}},\,
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,2,n}^{\mathrm{diag\text{-}G}}
    \bigr)
    \succeq
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R,n}^{\mathrm{loc\text{-}block}}
\end{equation*}
at every voxel. The three \glsentryshort{mm}-derived operands therefore share one exact spatial reduction and differ only in the within-voxel treatment: the coupling is retained exactly by the voxel block, dropped by the Jacobi diagonal, and bounded by the Gershgorin inflation. All three coincide at voxels whose incident targets carry no cross-modal weight, $c_{12,r}=0$ for every $r\in\mathcal{N}(n)$. The same extraction-versus-bounding choice recurs at the spatial level.

Simply extracting a diagonal from the non-separable Hessian does not preserve the bound~\eqref{eq:jacobian_majoriser}. The Gershgorin construction~\eqref{eq:diag_gersh_preconditioner} inflates along the modality index only and does not close the spatial majorisation gap. De Pierro's convexity construction instead gives a separable upper bound \cite{depierroModifiedExpectationMaximization1995a}, potentially at the cost of a more conservative step. No De Pierro model was implemented here.

\subsubsection{Lewis--Sendov block curvature}

The fourth construction starts from the exact Lewis--Sendov Hessian of the smoothed spectral penalty \cite{doi:10.1137/S089547980036838X}. If $H_{\mathcal R}=\nabla^2\mathcal R$ denotes the resulting image-space Hessian, the implemented approximation retains only locally assembled voxel blocks,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_{\mathcal R}^{\mathrm{LS\text{-}block}}
    =
    \operatorname{blkdiag}
    \left(
        \widehat H_{11}^{\mathrm{LS}},\ldots,\widehat H_{NN}^{\mathrm{LS}}
    \right),
    \qquad
    \widehat H_{nn}^{\mathrm{LS}}\approx[H_{\mathcal R}]_{nn}\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}.
    \label{eq:ls_block_preconditioner}
\end{equation}
It therefore retains \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} cross-curvature within each voxel but discards spatial off-diagonal blocks. In practice each voxel block is assembled per neighbourhood target: the Lewis--Sendov calculus is evaluated in local Jacobian coordinates, and the retained direction-diagonal blocks are mapped into modality space by the diagonal congruence
\begin{equation}
    T_{r,\ell}
    =
    \operatorname{diag}(a_{r,\ell})\,
    \Theta_{r,\ell}\,
    \operatorname{diag}(a_{r,\ell})
    =
    \bigl(a_{r,\ell}a_{r,\ell}^{\top}\bigr)\circ \Theta_{r,\ell},
    \label{eq:ls_block_pullback}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta_{r,\ell}$ is the local Hessian block for difference channel $\ell$ at target $r$ and the modality scalings $[a_{r,\ell}]_m$ combine the modality weights $\rho_{m,r}$ and the stencil step scaling with the square roots of the channel-$\ell$ diagonal entries of the operator Gram matrices $\Pi_{m,r}^{\top}\Pi_{m,r}$. Each contribution~\eqref{eq:ls_block_pullback} is the Hadamard product of a rank-one Gram matrix with a positive-semidefinite local block---$\Theta_{r,\ell}$ is a principal block of the local Hessian of the convex smoothed spectral penalty---hence positive semidefinite by the Schur product theorem. Accumulated over the targets incident on voxel $n$ and their difference channels, the assembled voxel blocks are therefore positive semidefinite before flooring. Because the scalings compress the cross-operator geometry to its diagonal energies, the assembled blocks approximate the exact voxel blocks $[H_{\mathcal R}]_{nn}$ rather than reproducing them exactly.

Evaluating the smoothed spectral Hessian requires the voxel-wise singular-value decompositions used by the Lewis--Sendov construction. Recomputing this block model once per epoch consequently has a non-negligible cost.

This construction is Newton-like rather than \gls{mm}-like: it builds its blocks from the exact local Hessian rather than reducing the quadratic majoriser above. Convexity gives $H_{\mathcal R}\succeq0$, but does not make these blocks invertible. Numerical flooring and, in the combined model, addition of positive data curvature make the practical inverse well defined and positive definite. The local Hessian of a non-quadratic function need not majorise that function, so this curvature model alone supplies no descent guarantee.

\subsubsection{Interaction with the non-negativity constraint}
\label{subsubsec:precond_constraint}

For a positive-definite preconditioner $\mathcal P$, the compatible projection onto the non-negative orthant is the metric projection
\begin{equation*}
    \Pi_{\mathbb{R}_+}^{\mathcal P^{-1}}(y)
    :=
    \underset{u\geq0}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;
    \tfrac12(u-y)^\top\mathcal P^{-1}(u-y).
\end{equation*}
When $\mathcal P$ is positive diagonal, this reduces to component-wise clipping. Off-diagonal \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} voxel blocks instead couple the constrained components, so the metric projection is generally not clipping. Consequently, the implemented block-preconditioned step followed by the Euclidean clipping in~\eqref{eq:optimisation_projected_update} is not the standard variable-metric projected-gradient step, and its usual guarantee does not transfer. This observation does not imply that the block method must fail; it identifies a gap between the implemented update and that convergence theory.

\subsection{Alternative Combination: Lehmer Family}

The inverse sum belongs to a broader matrix Lehmer family. For voxel-wise symmetric positive-definite preconditioners $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}$, define
\begin{equation}
    T=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1/2}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1/2},
    \qquad
    L_p(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D})
    =\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{1/2}f_p(T)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{1/2},
    \qquad
    f_p(t)=\frac{t^p+1}{t^{p-1}+1},
    \label{eq:matrix_lehmer_combination}
\end{equation}
where $f_p(T)$ is evaluated spectrally. The implemented form is $\tfrac12L_p$; at $p=0$ it reproduces the inverse sum exactly,
\begin{equation}
    \tfrac12L_0(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D})
    =
    \left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm D}^{-1}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal R}^{-1}\right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:lehmer_parallel_equivalence}
\end{equation}
Thus $p=0$ has the conditional additive-majoriser interpretation above; other Lehmer orders are alternative combination rules without that guarantee.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:optimisation_experiments}

The experiments compare the alternatives defined above while keeping the reconstruction objective fixed. Subset scheduling is assessed with one curvature model; preconditioning is then assessed with one subset schedule. These are controlled comparisons, not a joint search over all settings.

\subsection{Common Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}

The optimisation experiments used an anthropomorphic phantom scanned on the Mediso AnyScan at the National Physical Laboratory. \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} were each partitioned into 18 subsets. Each candidate configuration was evaluated over 20 matched expected data-draw units with five stochastic realisations and initial step $\varsigma^{(0)}=1$. The 20-epoch folded, modality-separated runs used $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$; the subset experiment rescales this rate to match relaxation across schedules. A single 1000-epoch reconstruction of the same objective, using a Gershgorin-diagonal \gls{mm} preconditioner and slower relaxation decay ($\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=10^{-4}$), provided the reference image $x^\star$. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_convergence} reports the objective and iterate diagnostics supporting its effective stationarity relative to the best observed iterate.

For each stochastic run and each saved iteration, the \acrfull{nrmse} in region $R$ is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{NRMSE}(x_k,x^\star;R)
    =
    \frac{\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_{j\in R}(x_{k,j}-x^\star_j)^2}}
         {\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_{j\in R}(x^\star_j)^2}},
    \qquad N=|R|.
    \label{eq:optimisation_nrmse}
\end{equation}
The regions are evaluated separately over the whole image, the hot-sphere mask, the cold-sphere mask, and the liver compartment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_voi_overlay}. The whole-image region includes the low-activity background surrounding the phantom, and is therefore the most sensitive of the four to behaviour in voxels that carry no clinical information; the liver is the largest structure of interest and the region over which absorbed dose to normal tissue would be scored. The plotted centre line is the arithmetic mean of the five per-run \gls{nrmse}; the envelope is their pointwise minimum--maximum.

\subsection{Subset Selection}
\label{sec:optimisation_subset_selection}

The first experiment asks how subset organisation and prior scheduling affect progress towards the reference. It crosses the two data organisations with the three prior schedules, holding the curvature model fixed.

\subsubsection{Experiment design and work accounting}
\label{subsec:subset_selection_experiment_methods}

With 18 subsets per modality, $N_{\mathrm d}=36$ for separate organisation and $N_{\mathrm d}=18$ for paired organisation. All stochastic components were sampled uniformly with replacement. One matched expected data-draw unit represents 36 modality-specific data-subset evaluations: 18 paired data draws or 36 separate draws. For folded, sampled, and once-per-epoch scheduling, respectively, one expected unit requires $N_{\mathrm d}$, $2N_{\mathrm d}$, and $N_{\mathrm d}+1$ total stochastic updates. These are 18, 36, and 19 updates for paired organisation, and 36, 72, and 37 for separate organisation; this matches expected data exposure, not wall-clock cost or snapshot work.

Let $L_q$ denote the number of stochastic updates per matched unit for configuration $q$. Since $k$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:optimisation_step_size} counts stochastic updates, the relaxation rate was normalised as
\[
    \eta_{\mathrm{relax}}^{(q)}
    =0.002\,\frac{36}{L_q}.
\]
At unit $p$, $k_q=L_qp$ and hence $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}^{(q)}k_q=0.002\times36p$; every curve therefore has the same step size at a given matched unit. The plotted axis is matched by expected data exposure and relaxation history, but not by total update count or computation. All configurations used the same smooth objective and the \gls{mm} Jacobi-diagonal curvature preconditioner of Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_prior_preconditioning}, and were assessed with the common 20-unit protocol in Section~\ref{subsec:optimisation_evaluation_protocol}.

\subsubsection{Results and interpretation}

Figure~\ref{fig:optim_subset_organisation} separates the six configurations mainly by prior schedule. The folded runs reduce mean \gls{nrmse} over the tested 20-unit interval; separate-folded attains the lowest mean \gls{nrmse} at unit 20 in every region for both modalities, with paired-folded close behind. The separate-folded \gls{pet} whole-image \gls{nrmse} curve has a transient jump near unit six before recovering. Neither the sampled nor once-per-epoch schedule shows a net \gls{nrmse} reduction over the same interval.

The prior correction gives one precise difference between the schedules. Let $\Delta_{\mathcal R}=\nabla\mathcal R(\bm z^{(k)})-\nabla\mathcal R(\widetilde{\bm z})$. Its contribution is $\Delta_{\mathcal R}$ at every folded update, either zero or $2\Delta_{\mathcal R}$ under sampled scheduling, and either zero or $(N_{\mathrm d}+1)\Delta_{\mathcal R}$ under once-per-epoch scheduling. Although its expectation is $\Delta_{\mathcal R}$ in all cases, the conditional covariance of the prior correction is zero, $\Delta_{\mathcal R}\Delta_{\mathcal R}^{\mathsf T}$, and $N_{\mathrm d}\Delta_{\mathcal R}\Delta_{\mathcal R}^{\mathsf T}$, respectively. The resulting large intermittent correction is therefore a plausible contributor to the poorer finite-interval behaviour of the once-per-epoch schedule, although the experiment does not isolate its effect from the accompanying change in data-component sampling.

The relaxation normalisation rules out different step-size reduction with matched-unit count as the explanation. With the smooth objective, preconditioner, expected data-exposure axis and relaxation history fixed, the observed differences arise from the component and subset organisation, including how the prior correction is scheduled. Total update counts still differ---within the folded schedule, for example, separate organisation performs 36 updates per matched unit and paired organisation 18---so the comparison is not compute-matched and establishes no wall-clock advantage.

\subsection{Preconditioner Comparison}

The next experiment asks whether incorporating prior curvature improves the updates, and whether retaining within-voxel coupling justifies its cost. It holds the subset organisation and prior schedule fixed while changing the preconditioner.

\subsubsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{subsec:preconditioner_experiment_methods}

The comparison uses modality-separated subsets and the folded prior schedule, which gave the lowest final mean \gls{nrmse} in the subset experiment. All methods use the common protocol above, with the same objective, initialisation and long-run reference. Sensitivity-derived support masks are enabled with relative threshold $10^{-3}$, and the data component is smoothed with a $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ Gaussian \gls{fwhm}.

The preconditioner is updated once per epoch and held fixed between updates. This limits curvature-computation cost, particularly for the Lewis--Sendov construction, but means the preconditioner becomes progressively stale within each epoch.

The long-run reference provides $x^\star$ for the \gls{nrmse} calculation rather than an additional candidate method. Since every method addresses the same objective, the preconditioner changes the optimisation metric rather than the objective or its set of minimisers, so a reference generated under one metric is a common comparison target for all. For the block methods, whose clipped update lies outside variable-metric projected-gradient theory (Section~\ref{subsubsec:precond_constraint}), approach to $x^\star$ is an empirical observation rather than a guaranteed limit.

\section{Interpretation and Limitations}

\subsection{Similarity of the prior-aware models}
\label{subsubsec:prior_model_similarity}

The three \glsentryshort{mm}-derived operands originate from the same frozen curvature and share one exact spatial reduction; they differ only in how within-voxel \gls{pet}/\gls{spect} coupling is handled --- retained exactly by the voxel block, dropped by the Jacobi diagonal, upper-bounded before extraction by the Gershgorin inflation. Their near-coincidence in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_preconditioners} therefore measures the influence of that coupling on the optimisation metric, not differences in spatial approximation. Two mechanisms plausibly damp it. First, the cross-modal weight $c_{12,r}$ is appreciable only where both modalities carry local gradients that are neither negligible nor orthogonal; such modality-discordant edge voxels form a thin shell of a volumetric \gls{voi}, and the three operands coincide at voxels whose incident targets all have $c_{12,r}=0$. Second, every prior operand enters through the inverse sum~\eqref{eq:parallel_sum_combination}, so wherever the data curvature dominates the denominator, residual differences between prior operands are attenuated further. That the Lewis--Sendov block model also tracks the \glsentryshort{mm} family suggests the curvature source is likewise not the limiting factor over this interval. The voxel-wise distribution of $|c_{12,n}|/c_{mm,n}$ was not measured, so this reading remains an interpretation; recording it at a single preconditioner refresh would test it directly.

\subsection{Separable majorisation as future work}

The implemented Jacobi diagonal is the exact diagonal of the chosen frozen surrogate; the outstanding theoretical gap is majorisation. A De Pierro separable construction could provide an upper bound, although the bound may be more conservative. A controlled comparison should assess majorisation slack, reconstruction convergence and computational cost under a common stopping criterion; no such benchmark was performed here.

\subsection{Modality-dependent prior/data curvature balance}
\label{subsec:modality_curvature_balance}

A plausible explanation for the modality dependence in Figure~\ref{fig:optim_preconditioners} is the balance between the data and prior curvature. For the Jacobi-diagonal model, a useful voxel-wise diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\widehat H_{\mathcal R,m,n}}{\widehat H_{\mathrm D,m,n}}
    =
    \frac{\left[H_{\mathcal R}^{\mathrm{frozen}}\right]_{(m,n),(m,n)}\,(z_{m,n}+\epsilon_x)}{s_{m,n}} .
    \label{eq:curvature_ratio}
\end{equation}
The data curvature contains no explicit $\rho$ dependence, whereas the prior diagonal accumulates $\rho_{m,r}^2\,c_{mm,r}$ over the incident stencil. In a scalar or effectively uncoupled limit, this gives intuitive linear scaling with $\rho$ for large directional gradients and quadratic scaling near a flat region. For the coupled $2\times2$ problem, however, $c_{mm,r}$ depends on both modality columns and their cross-coupling, so these limits do not bound the net scaling. The ratio in~\eqref{eq:curvature_ratio} was not measured in the experiment and is offered only as local, diagonal intuition.

The weights $\rho_{m,n}$ were selected in Chapter~\ref{chapter:wdtnv} to balance gradients entering the nuclear norm, independently of the modality sensitivities. It is therefore plausible, but not established, that the different \gls{pet} and \gls{spect} trajectories reflect different prior/data curvature ratios. An untested extension would introduce a separate modality scaling for the preconditioner,
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda
    =
    \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\mathrm{PET}},\lambda_{\mathrm{SPECT}}),
    \qquad
    \mathcal P_{\Lambda}
    =
    \left(
        \widehat H_{\mathrm D}
        +
        \Lambda^{1/2}\widehat H_{\mathcal R}\Lambda^{1/2}
    \right)^{-1},
    \qquad \lambda_{m}>0,
    \label{eq:modality_precond_weight}
\end{equation}
which reduces to modality-wise curvature scaling when the operands are diagonal. This changes the optimisation metric without changing the objective, unlike retuning $\rho_{m,n}$. The choice of $\Lambda$ was not investigated here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:optimisation_conclusion}

Joint multi-bed reconstruction allowed the edge-preserving prior to act across the bed overlap and did not exhibit the overlap artefacts visible in the qualitative stitched example. In the one-bed subset experiment, all schedules used the same smooth objective and an unbiased uniform-sampling \gls{svrg} estimator. We rescaled the relaxation rate from the folded, modality-separated setting $\eta_{\mathrm{relax}}=0.002$, giving every configuration the same step size at a given matched unit. On that expected data-exposure axis, the folded schedules reduced \gls{nrmse} over 20 units and the intermittent schedules did not. Since the total update counts differ, this comparison does not establish a wall-clock or compute-efficiency advantage.

In the preconditioner experiment, prior-aware scaling kept count-limited \gls{pet} bounded over the same 20-pass interval. Data-only \gls{bsrem} gave comparable or slightly lower \gls{spect} \gls{nrmse}, and no prior-curvature construction was uniformly best. Since the block models cost approximately eight to ten times more per epoch than the diagonal models without a consistent convergence advantage, the diagonal constructions were preferable for the tested implementation. The local curvature ratio may help explain the modality dependence, but it was not measured. The block-valid scaling in~\eqref{eq:modality_precond_weight} therefore remains an untested extension.

The $p=0.01$ Lehmer result was only slightly below the parallel-sum \gls{nrmse}, and it also changes the effective step scale. Chapter~\ref{chapter:paper} retains the Lewis--Sendov block-diagonal/$p=0.01$ setting because that application protocol was fixed before the broader benchmark, not because these results establish it as universally optimal.
